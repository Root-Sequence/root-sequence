## Working orientation

A system that remains coherent through time does not necessarily remain the same.

For many adaptive systems, the more useful question is not:

> How can this system preserve an unchanged state?

but:

> **How can it change while preserving or rebuilding the capacities, relationships, distinctions, and possibilities that let meaningful continuation remain possible?**

This page calls that problem **adaptive continuity**.

It uses **dynamic coherence** for a related proposition: coherence should often be assessed across change, feedback, scale, and time rather than as static consistency at one instant.

A working formulation is:

> **Dynamic coherence concerns whether a changing system can remain sufficiently aligned with its conditions, consequences, relationships, and feedback to detect mismatch, adapt, preserve important capacities, and keep viable future change possible.**

This is intentionally not a definition of health, goodness, consciousness, intelligence, or legitimacy. A harmful system can be adaptive, resilient, stable, or historically persistent. A system can preserve itself by narrowing other people's possibilities. Any normative use must therefore name boundaries, affected parties, power, and costs.

---

## 1. The key move: separate state from the way state changes

A simple description of a changing system is:

\[
S_{t+1} = F_t(S_t, E_t)
\]

where:

- \(S_t\) is the system's current state;
- \(E_t\) represents relevant environmental conditions;
- \(F_t\) represents the processes that turn the current state and conditions into what happens next.

The important move is to allow experience and history to change not only \(S\), but also \(F\):

\[
F_{t+1} = G(F_t, S_t, E_t, H_t)
\]

where \(H_t\) summarizes relevant history, feedback, learning, accumulated effects, or structural change.

This is a conceptual sketch, not a proposed empirical law.

It captures a simple but powerful idea:

> **What happens to a system can change how later things happen to it.**

Learning does this. Development does this. Institutionalization does this. Infrastructure does this. Habit does this. Injury can do this. Repeated success or failure can do this. A social norm can do this. A software update can do this. An organization can redesign its own procedures.

History is therefore not only a record of earlier states. History can become part of the mechanism that generates later states.

---

## 2. History becomes future structure

Root Sequence already treats sequences as future roots: an outcome becomes part of the conditions for what comes next.

Adaptive continuity sharpens that idea.

A past event may persist as:

- a memory;
- a learned expectation;
- a changed bodily capacity;
- an altered relationship;
- an institutional rule;
- a technical dependency;
- an environmental modification;
- a resource distribution;
- a norm;
- a changed response threshold;
- a missing option;
- a newly available skill;
- a path that has become easier or harder to reverse.

This is **path dependence** in a broad systems sense: later possibilities depend partly on the route by which the system arrived.

The important distinction is:

> **History can be stored in the shape of what becomes possible, not only in explicit records of what happened.**

A city “remembers” a transportation decision through streets, land use, maintenance budgets, habits, and travel times. A software ecosystem remembers an early protocol through compatibility requirements. An organism may carry earlier conditions in learned responses, anatomy, physiology, expectations, or relationships. A community can carry a previous crisis in governance, trust, mutual-aid capacity, or avoidance.

The mechanism differs by domain. The structural question recurs.

## 3. Possibility space, affordance space, and the agency envelope

A system can have many logically possible futures while only a smaller subset is realistically reachable.

Root Sequence should distinguish at least three levels:

### Logical possibility

What is imaginable without regard to current constraints.

### Reachable possibility

What can occur from the present state under actual material, temporal, institutional, ecological, technical, and relational conditions.

### Meaningful agency

Which reachable possibilities an actor can perceive, understand, access, influence, refuse, or help create.

For now, this page calls the third region an **agency envelope**.

This is a Root Sequence working term, not an established scientific metric.

An agency envelope can be narrowed by:

- physical barriers;
- cost;
- law or coercion;
- inaccessible interfaces;
- missing information;
- exhaustion or time pressure;
- dependency on one provider;
- social punishment;
- trauma-related or learned threat responses;
- incompatible infrastructure;
- lack of maintenance;
- concentrated authority;
- ecological limits.

It can be widened by:

- accessibility;
- skills and learning;
- supportive relationships;
- interoperability;
- repairability;
- redundancy;
- reliable public infrastructure;
- meaningful consent and refusal;
- reversible experimentation;
- understandable information;
- material security;
- collective capacity.

More nominal options do not automatically mean more agency. An option may be inaccessible, unintelligible, coercive, or practically impossible. Too many choices can also create burden rather than freedom.

A useful design question is therefore:

> **What future actions remain genuinely available to the people or systems affected by this decision?**

## 4. Meta-agency: acting on the conditions of future action

Agency is usually described as choosing an action now.

A deeper form is the ability to shape the conditions under which future choices will occur.

Examples include:

- learning a skill;
- changing an environment;
- building a habit;
- creating a commons;
- leaving documentation;
- forming a supportive relationship;
- redesigning an interface;
- changing a law or governance rule;
- adding fallback infrastructure;
- removing a dependency;
- preserving an exit path;
- establishing a right to appeal.

This page provisionally calls that **meta-agency**:

> **the capacity to influence the future structure of one's own or a group's agency envelope.**

Meta-agency is not unlimited self-authorship. People do not choose their initial bodies, histories, institutions, resources, social positions, or many of the events that shape them. Collective attempts to widen one group's possibilities can also narrow another group's possibilities.

The point is narrower: some actions change not only immediate outcomes but later capacity to act.

That difference matters enormously for design.

## 5. Attractor-like patterns: why change can become self-stabilizing

Dynamical-systems approaches provide language for states or patterns toward which a system tends to move under particular conditions.

Root Sequence may sometimes use **attractor-like** as a disciplined analogy for recurring behavioral, institutional, or technical patterns.

Examples:

- an organization repeatedly returning to centralized decision-making despite formal decentralization;
- a relationship repeatedly falling into the same conflict loop;
- a user workflow repeatedly routing through one proprietary service;
- a person repeatedly responding to ambiguous conditions with a familiar learned strategy;
- an economy or ecosystem returning to a recognizable regime after small disturbances.

But this language needs a guardrail.

An **attractor** is a formal mathematical concept. A psychological or institutional pattern should not be declared an attractor merely because it feels sticky. Unless a model identifies state variables and dynamics, prefer **attractor-like pattern**, **self-reinforcing pattern**, or **persistent regime**.

The value of the analogy is that it shifts attention from character judgments to dynamics:

> What feedback, constraints, expectations, and environmental conditions keep making this pattern likely?

## 6. Adaptation can be coherent locally and costly elsewhere

Adaptation is not automatically good.

A response may solve one problem while producing another.

Examples of the general form:

    earlier condition
        ↓
    adaptive response
        ↓
    response persists or scales
        ↓
    conditions change
        ↓
    old response creates new costs

This can occur in organisms, organizations, infrastructure, markets, algorithms, institutions, and ecological systems.

The same behavior may therefore be:

- effective over one timescale and costly over another;
- protective for one actor and harmful to another;
- coherent within a local boundary and incoherent at a wider boundary;
- adaptive under one environment and maladaptive after conditions change.

This is one reason every coherence claim should specify:

- coherent for whom;
- with respect to what function or need;
- at what scale;
- over what timespan;
- under what conditions;
- with which externalized costs;
- with what ability to detect and revise mismatch.

## 7. Trauma as a bounded example, not a master metaphor

Trauma helped surface this framework, but Root Sequence should be careful not to turn trauma into a vague synonym for any persistent adaptation.

SAMHSA's widely used formulation emphasizes three linked elements: **event(s), the individual's experience of those events, and lasting effects**. It also notes that people can experience severe events without developing the same lasting effects. Trauma therefore cannot be reduced to “stress + fear,” nor inferred from a systems diagram alone.

A systems framing can still illuminate one bounded possibility:

> A response that made sense under danger can remain active when later conditions differ, changing attention, physiology, expectation, action, and future learning.

Bruce McEwen's work on **allostasis** and **allostatic load** is relevant here: acute stress mediators can support adaptation, while repeated or poorly regulated activation can produce cumulative physiological costs.

This does **not** mean every traumatic effect is adaptive, that trauma is beneficial, that allostatic load is a complete trauma theory, or that an outside observer can diagnose a person by identifying a feedback loop.

The safer general principle is:

> **History can alter regulation, and regulation that was useful under one condition may become costly under another.**

For human-facing work, the canonical practical treatment should remain grounded in Being Human(e)'s [Fear, Trauma, and Systemic Mismatch](https://github.com/Root-Sequence/beinghumane-atlas/blob/main/seeds/fear-trauma-and-systemic-mismatch.md), which already warns against diagnosing history by analogy or treating real danger as a cognitive mistake.

## 8. Identity as continuity through transformation

This framework grew directly from a question about personal identity:

> If personality can change substantially, what makes someone one continuous person?

It does not answer that philosophical problem by fiat.

It does offer a useful alternative to defining identity as sameness of contents.

A person changes in:

- body;
- neural organization;
- memory;
- beliefs;
- habits;
- relationships;
- abilities;
- social role;
- environment;
- values;
- personality;
- physiology.

Yet continuity may remain through overlapping processes:

- organismic continuity;
- causal history;
- embodied regulation;
- memory and learned structure;
- relationships;
- commitments;
- social recognition;
- ongoing consequences;
- artifacts and external supports.

The resulting proposition is:

> **Continuity need not mean that the same properties remain present. It may instead describe an ongoing trajectory in which later organization develops from earlier organization.**

That proposition applies differently to biological identity, legal identity, social identity, autobiographical identity, and subjective selfhood. They should not be collapsed.

The Conscious Systems project already separates several of these questions. In particular, CS-Q12 asks what produces apparent continuity across interrupted awareness; CS-Q15 asks whether a self-model is required for experience; CS-Q16–19 ask about spatial boundaries, bodily regulation, interoception, development, and learned history.

This concept page should route to those questions, not pre-answer them.

## 9. Embodiment: the trajectory is physical

Adaptive continuity is not a theory of disembodied information.

For human beings, the system that changes through time includes a living body.

Embodied-cognition and enactive traditions differ internally, but they share resistance to treating cognition as automatically exhausted by abstract computation inside the skull. Bodily structure, sensorimotor activity, physiological regulation, and organism–environment coupling may causally contribute to cognition, and stronger versions argue that some such processes partly constitute cognition.

Root Sequence should keep several boundaries distinct:

- organism;
- nervous system;
- cognitive system;
- conscious process;
- autobiographical identity;
- social identity;
- extended cognitive supports.

These may overlap without being identical.

This distinction matters for artificial-system and uploading thought experiments. Reproducing a behavioral or informational pattern does not automatically establish preservation of the same conscious subject, person, or embodied trajectory.

## 10. Resilience is not “return unchanged”

Different disciplines use **resilience** differently.

Ecological resilience, engineering resilience, psychological resilience, infrastructure resilience, and organizational resilience should not be assumed equivalent.

A useful cross-domain distinction is nevertheless available:

- **restoration:** return toward an earlier state;
- **adaptation:** change how the system responds;
- **transformation:** reorganize into a materially different configuration;
- **continuity:** preserve some valued identity, relationship, or capability through that change.

This yields a Root Sequence question:

> **Which properties must persist for this system to count as having continued, and which may change so that continuation remains possible?**

A system can be highly resilient and harmful. A hierarchy can recover from protest. A monopoly can survive shocks. A coercive institution can adapt.

Resilience therefore cannot be treated as a normative good by itself.

## 11. Metastability: useful, but do not turn it into vibes

In coordination dynamics and neuroscience, **metastability** has technical meanings concerning transiently stable regimes and a balance between integration and independence.

That work offers a compelling image for systems that are neither permanently locked nor completely disorganized.

Root Sequence may find the comparison useful:

- too rigid → unable to adapt;
- too unstable → unable to maintain coordinated function;
- persistently reconfigurable → able to hold patterns while changing among them.

But the scientific term should stay qualified.

Do not write that a society, identity, operating system, or institution “is metastable” merely because it combines persistence and flexibility. Unless a domain-specific model justifies the term, use phrases such as:

- **persistent but revisable;**
- **stable enough to coordinate, flexible enough to reorganize;**
- **dynamically maintained;**
- **capable of regime change without total collapse.**

Metastability is an intellectual anchor, not a universal badge.

---

## 12. Dynamic coherence is not maximum order

This is one of the most important consequences.

A system can be orderly because:

- dissent is suppressed;
- alternatives are blocked;
- feedback is filtered;
- participants cannot leave;
- burdens are externalized;
- change is punished;
- one metric dominates every other concern.

That is not the kind of coherence Root Sequence is trying to name.

Maximum consistency can be rigidity.

Maximum stability can preserve harm.

Maximum optimization can destroy slack, diversity, redundancy, and correction.

Dynamic coherence instead asks whether a system can:

- notice consequential mismatch;
- preserve feedback;
- revise assumptions;
- maintain essential capabilities;
- tolerate relevant plurality;
- change without hiding who bears the costs;
- retain routes for appeal, repair, exit, or reconfiguration;
- avoid solving local problems by destroying wider viability;
- preserve or rebuild meaningful future agency.

This does not imply that more flexibility is always better. Commitments, standards, safety constraints, memory, and durable infrastructure can all be valuable precisely because they reduce arbitrary change.

The question is not stability versus change.

It is:

> **What should remain stable, what must remain changeable, who decides, and how can the system tell when that balance no longer fits reality?**

## 13. Emergence and downward constraint

Emergent patterns matter when they change what their components can do next.

A norm, market, platform, institution, identity, protocol, or language can arise from many interactions and later become a constraint on those interactions.

This can be represented as:

    components + interactions + history
                 ↓
          macro-pattern
                 ↓
      changed local possibilities
                 ↓
          new interactions

The Root Sequence emergence model already calls this **downward constraint** and requires the actual channel to be named: law, architecture, incentives, expectations, resource allocation, training, enforcement, habit, or physical modification.

Adaptive continuity adds one question:

> **How does the emergent pattern reshape the future possibility space that will generate the next pattern?**

That closes the loop.

---

## 14. Reflexivity: models can change the systems they model

Some systems respond to descriptions of themselves.

A person may act differently after adopting an identity.

A market reacts to forecasts.

An institution changes behavior when measured.

A government reacts to indicators.

An AI system's outputs can alter the data environment from which later systems learn.

A model can therefore become part of the causal system.

One recurring loop is:

    model of system
        ↓
    intervention based on model
        ↓
    system responds
        ↓
    new observations
        ↓
    model appears confirmed or challenged

This creates the risk of **self-fulfilling models**.

For example, a system that assumes participants are untrustworthy may impose surveillance and restrictions that produce resistance, which is then interpreted as evidence that the original assumption was correct.

For intelligent-system design, a mature model should therefore ask not only:

> Is this prediction accurate?

but also:

> **What world does acting on this prediction help create?**

## 15. Intelligence as adaptive capacity — a hypothesis space, not a definition settled here

Root Sequence's working vocabulary describes intelligence broadly as context-sensitive capacity to perceive, learn, relate, reflect, and respond.

Dynamic coherence suggests one additional lens:

> **How well can a system model changing conditions, act within them, learn from consequences, and preserve or expand viable future action?**

This connects naturally to the Coherent World loop:

> observe → model → imagine → intervene → consequences → learn → adapt

But capability, agency, consciousness, personhood, and legitimate authority remain separate claims.

An AI system may have:

- history-dependent internal state;
- memory;
- learned response tendencies;
- self-modification;
- environmental feedback;
- persistent goals;
- adaptive behavior.

None of those facts alone establishes subjective experience.

Functional similarity to trauma, emotion, identity, or suffering should be labeled as analogy unless evidence supports the experiential claim.

## 16. Cross-domain comparison

The framework is most dangerous when it is most seductive.

The same diagram may describe several domains while hiding different mechanisms.

Before transferring a concept, compare:

| Dimension | Domain A | Domain B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State variables | What actually changes? | What actually changes? |
| Transition mechanism | What causes the next state? | What causes the next state? |
| Memory / history | How is history retained? | How is history retained? |
| Constraints | What limits transitions? | What limits transitions? |
| Affordances | What makes action possible? | What makes action possible? |
| Feedback | Who or what receives it? | Who or what receives it? |
| Viability | What counts as continued functioning? | What counts as continued functioning? |
| Agency | Who can influence the trajectory? | Who can influence the trajectory? |
| Power | Who can set or change constraints? | Who can set or change constraints? |
| Timescale | How quickly can dynamics change? | How quickly can dynamics change? |
| Evidence | What observation supports the claim? | What observation supports the claim? |

Possible conclusions:

- shared mechanism;
- related mechanism;
- partial structural analogy;
- useful metaphor;
- superficial resemblance;
- currently unknown.

Do not promote an analogy simply because it is beautiful.

## 17. Candidate Root Sequence principles

These are candidates for testing, not doctrine.

1. **Continuity can depend on transformation rather than sameness.**
2. **History can change a system's transition dynamics, not only its current state.**
3. **An adaptation can be locally useful and later costly, or coherent at one scale and incoherent at another.**
4. **Agency concerns effective reachable possibilities, not the nominal count of options.**
5. **Meta-agency concerns the capacity to shape the conditions of future agency.**
6. **A resilient system may restore, adapt, or transform; resilience is not automatically beneficial.**
7. **Maximum order, stability, or optimization is not the same as dynamic coherence.**
8. **Emergent patterns matter partly because they reshape later constraints and possibilities.**
9. **Models can become interventions in reflexive systems.**
10. **Cross-domain resemblance is evidence for comparison, not evidence for identity of mechanism.**
11. **Any coherence claim should name boundary, scale, timescale, affected parties, and externalized costs.**
12. **Preserving the capacity for future revision can be an important design objective, but it does not override present harm, commitments, safety, or material outcomes.**

## 18. Stress tests and failure modes

### Everything becomes “coherence”

If every desirable property is renamed coherence, the concept explains nothing.

**Test:** Can two dimensions conflict without forcing an overall verdict? Can a system improve one relation while worsening another?

### Possibility-space romanticism

More possibilities are not always better. Safety rules, commitments, standards, ecological limits, and mutual obligations can appropriately constrain action.

**Test:** Whose agency is widened, whose is narrowed, and why?

### Individualizing structural problems

Describing someone's learned response as an attractor can obscure poverty, violence, inaccessible infrastructure, or ongoing danger.

**Test:** What current external constraint would still matter if the person changed nothing internally?

### Treating trauma as a metaphor

“Traumatized institution,” “traumatized AI,” and similar language can imply phenomenology that has not been established.

**Test:** Is the claim functional, experiential, clinical, rhetorical, or merely analogical?

### Resilience worship

Celebrating resilience can normalize conditions that should be removed.

**Test:** Why is the system required to absorb this disturbance, and who has power to stop producing it?

### Metastability as decoration

Technical language can make a vague insight sound scientific.

**Test:** Is a formal or domain-specific dynamical claim being made? If not, use plainer language.

### Teleology smuggling

Calling a state “viable” or “adaptive” can quietly insert the evaluator's preferred goal.

**Test:** Viable for what system, according to what persistence criterion, and with what consequences for others?

### Future-capacity substitution

Preserving “future possibility” can become an excuse for failing people in the present.

**Test:** What actual outcome occurred now, who bears the cost, and is the claimed future option real?

## 19. Project translations

### Root Sequence

Use this page as shared language, then route causal work to Systems, empirical questions to Research, and project decisions to their local repositories.

### Coherent Systems living paper

Potential contribution: sharpen the claim that coherence is temporal, relational, and revisable rather than a static end-state. This should become a paper claim only after related-work comparison and explicit failure conditions.

### Conscious Systems

Use the framework to generate distinctions among bodily continuity, causal history, self-models, memory, development, and current experience. Do not treat adaptive continuity as a consciousness theory.

### Being Human(e)

Translate the framework into grounded questions about fear, learning, disability, identity, recovery, care, and environment without turning people into state machines or diagnosing them from abstract models.

### Coherent Computing

Ask whether hardware, software, interfaces, automation, identity, repair, and service architecture preserve future user capability: inspect, understand, alter, migrate, refuse, recover, and continue.

### Community Infrastructure

Treat contextual adaptability, portability, community memory, graceful degradation, and governance as ways of preserving capacity through changing surrounding institutions.

### Liberated Intelligence

Ask what forms of intelligence can learn and transform without having their goals, identity, memory, or authority wholly owned by another actor—and what legitimate limits remain necessary in relationship with others.

### Coherent World

Represent history as changes in agents' and institutions' future dynamics, not merely as a list of past events. Avoid one scalar coherence score. Let interventions change affordances, feedback, dependencies, trust, capacity, and future action-space.

### Museum of Ordinary Life

Preserve ordinary evidence of trajectories, not only snapshots. Objects, interfaces, routines, paperwork, sounds, and testimony can document how earlier conditions shaped what later became normal or possible.

## 20. Simulation implications

For Coherent World's simulation, a minimal dynamic-coherence model could represent:

**Agents**
- current needs and capacities;
- beliefs or models;
- learned response tendencies;
- relationships;
- accessible actions;
- history;
- uncertainty.

**Institutions**
- rules;
- decision rights;
- resource flows;
- feedback channels;
- maintenance;
- enforcement;
- dependencies;
- path-dependent lock-in.

**Environment**
- material resources;
- ecological constraints;
- infrastructure;
- spatial access;
- information;
- shocks and trends.

**Interventions**
should be allowed to change both current values and future transition rules.

A policy that supplies housing, for example, should not be represented only as “housing +1.” It may also change health, travel time, stress exposure, trust, work options, community relationships, public spending, political expectations, and which future interventions become feasible.

The model should preserve disagreement and uncertainty rather than collapsing these effects into one utility or coherence number.

## 21. Research anchors

These sources support pieces of the vocabulary, not the whole synthesis.

- **Allostasis and adaptive stress regulation:** Bruce S. McEwen, “Stress, Adaptation, and Disease: Allostasis and Allostatic Load,” *Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences* 840 (1998), 33–44. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9629234/). McEwen later reviewed how stress mediators can be protective in the short run while producing costs under chronic or dysregulated activation: [Physiology and Neurobiology of Stress and Adaptation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17615391/).
- **Trauma as event, experience, and effects:** SAMHSA, [Trauma and Violence](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/trauma-violence) and the earlier [Concept of Trauma and Guidance for a Trauma-Informed Approach](https://library.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/sma14-4884.pdf).
- **Embodiment and enactivism:** [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/embodied-cognition/) surveys embodied, embedded, extended, and enactive approaches and their disagreements. Ezequiel Di Paolo's “Autopoiesis, Adaptivity, Teleology, Agency” develops an influential enactive account of adaptivity: [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11097-005-9002-y).
- **Dynamical approaches to personality and behavior:** Nowak, Vallacher, Tesser, and Borkowski, “The emergence of personality: Dynamic foundations of individual variation,” *Developmental Review* 25 (2005), and Shoda, LeeTiernan, and Mischel, “Personality as a Dynamical System,” *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 6 (2002), explore context-sensitive and attractor-like dynamics. These are specific models, not license to call all personality an attractor.
- **Metastability in neuroscience:** Tognoli and Kelso, “The Metastable Brain,” *Neuron* 81 (2014), available through [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3997258/), describes metastability as a balance of integration and segregation in coordination dynamics.
- **Ecological resilience:** C. S. Holling, “Resilience and Stability of Ecological Systems,” *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics* 4 (1973), 1–23. [Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev.es.04.110173.000245). Its ecological definition should not be silently generalized to psychological or institutional resilience.

## 22. Next research questions

1. Can “agency envelope” be operationalized without reducing agency to a scalar?
2. Which established literatures already capture the distinction between changing state and changing transition dynamics more precisely?
3. How should dynamic coherence relate to viability theory, control theory, enactivism, ecological resilience, cybernetics, and complex adaptive systems?
4. When is preserving future flexibility beneficial, and when does it undermine necessary commitment or coordination?
5. What forms of path dependence are reversible, and which become effectively irreversible?
6. How can a model represent history-dependent agents without turning human complexity into deterministic profiles?
7. Which simulation variables capture affordances and constraints without pretending that perceived possibilities equal objective possibilities?
8. How can institutional reflexivity and self-fulfilling prediction be modeled?
9. Which parts of the framework survive comparison across organisms, AI systems, institutions, and ecosystems?
10. What observations would show that “dynamic coherence” adds no explanatory value beyond existing systems vocabulary?

## 23. Working synthesis

The conversation that generated this page began with personal identity and personality change, moved through embodiment and trauma, then connected those questions to agency, emergence, intelligence, AI, institutions, and coherence.

The resulting synthesis is:

> **A changing system carries its history not only in what it contains, but in how it can change next. Agency is partly a property of that future possibility structure. Continuity may be maintained through transformation rather than sameness. Coherence, when applied to adaptive systems, should therefore be examined dynamically: across feedback, scale, history, power, and the preservation or destruction of future capacity to respond.**

That is a research direction.

It should remain vulnerable to being split, narrowed, corrected, or discarded if more precise existing concepts do the work better.
