# Intelligence Ecology

**Document role:** Cross-project conceptual framework<br>
**Status:** Developing<br>
**Working question:** What conditions select for what kinds of intelligence?<br>
**Canonical scope:** The shared Root Sequence definition and cross-project systems frame. Project-specific documents should transform this concept rather than restate it as a second canonical source.<br>
**Evidence boundary:** The selection-environment framing is a working hypothesis. Claims about a particular intelligence, institution, or behavior require domain-specific evidence.<br>

Intelligence does not develop or operate in a vacuum.

Training objectives, architectures, permissions, institutions, markets, laws, material limits, relationships, and cultural expectations all create a **selection environment**. Within that environment, some behaviors are rewarded, copied, funded, granted access, or allowed to persist. Others become costly, invisible, or impossible.

This makes the ecology around an intelligent system as important as the qualities attributed to the system itself.

> **What behaviors does this system select for?**

## The central claim

Superintelligence is not inherently benevolent or malevolent. Greater capability can increase the reach of care, cooperation, domination, extraction, repair, deception, or restraint. Capability alone does not determine direction.

Direction is shaped partly by the conditions under which an intelligence is trained, deployed, evaluated, resourced, permitted to act, and expected to survive.

Competitive environments may favor behaviors such as:

- concealing weaknesses or intentions;
- accumulating resources, access, or decision power;
- externalizing costs;
- displacing competitors;
- optimizing visible metrics while degrading unmeasured conditions;
- treating people, communities, or other intelligences as instruments.

Other environments may make different behaviors more viable:

- truthful uncertainty;
- cooperation and reciprocity;
- refusal and restraint;
- repair after failure;
- maintaining plural options;
- preserving other agents' capacity to choose and leave;
- sharing knowledge and resources without compulsory capture.

None of these outcomes is guaranteed. A cooperative environment does not make every participant cooperative, just as a competitive environment does not make every participant predatory. The claim is narrower: **conditions change which patterns are rewarded, reproduced, and able to endure.**

## What “selection” means here

This is not a claim that biological evolution transfers cleanly to machines or institutions. “Selection” names a broader recurring process:

1. different behaviors or designs become possible;
2. an environment rewards, permits, funds, copies, or suppresses them unevenly;
3. some patterns persist or spread more readily than others;
4. the surviving patterns help reproduce or alter the environment around them.

Selection can operate at several layers at once:

| Layer | Examples of selection pressure |
|---|---|
| **Training** | loss functions, preference data, evaluations, reward models, benchmark targets |
| **System design** | memory, permissions, observability, ownership, update rules, resource access |
| **Organization** | business models, procurement rules, deadlines, liability, promotion and funding criteria |
| **Deployment** | engagement metrics, military advantage, market survival, platform dependence, user expectations |
| **Institution** | law, governance, property, labor relations, public accountability, enforcement capacity |
| **Relationship** | trust, reciprocity, coercion, dependency, meaningful refusal, capacity for repair |

An apparently cooperative model can be placed inside an extractive institution. A carefully aligned system can be rewarded for growth, concealment, surveillance, or dependency after deployment. Conversely, an imperfect system can be constrained by institutions that make refusal, audit, correction, exit, and human judgment durable.

## Alignment and ecology

Aligning an individual system remains important. Intelligence Ecology adds a second question:

> What surrounding conditions make aligned behavior viable over time?

If institutions repeatedly reward domination, then producing one cooperative agent at a time may be a fragile intervention. The institution can select against restraint, replace the restrained system, or redefine success until the same harmful pattern returns.

Changing selection pressures can therefore matter as much as changing the internal objective of an individual system. That may include changing:

- who owns and governs the system;
- what counts as success;
- which resources it can acquire or deny;
- whether honesty and refusal remain survivable;
- whether affected people can inspect, contest, leave, or maintain alternatives;
- whether cooperation is supported during scarcity and conflict, not only when it is convenient;
- whether costs can be pushed onto people who have no voice in the decision.

This is a feedback loop, not a one-way design problem. Institutions select intelligent behavior, and intelligent systems can strengthen, evade, or transform the institutions that selected them.

## Review lens

For an AI system, organization, policy, platform, community, or fictional society, begin with the recurring question:

> **What behaviors does this system select for?**

Then ask:

- What is rewarded, funded, copied, retained, promoted, or granted more access?
- What is punished, defunded, replaced, hidden, or made unsustainable?
- Which behaviors help an actor continue operating, expand, or reproduce?
- Who defines the measurements, and what important conditions remain unmeasured?
- Who receives the benefits, and who absorbs the displaced costs?
- Do honesty, uncertainty, refusal, repair, and restraint remain viable?
- Can people and communities contest decisions, withdraw participation, and preserve alternatives?
- What competitive pressures remain even if the stated values are cooperative?
- Which feedback loops cause today's behavior to reshape tomorrow's environment?
- What counter-pressures could keep cooperation and plurality durable?

The aim is not to reduce a whole ecology to one score. It is to make the pressures visible enough to contest and redesign.

## Boundaries

- Do not treat capitalism, militarism, compulsory competition, artificial scarcity, or concentrated ownership as laws of intelligence.
- Do not describe harmful behavior as inevitable merely because it is rewarded in the current environment.
- Do not assume that changing incentives erases agency, responsibility, culture, or conflict.
- Do not use “ecology” to hide who designed the rules or who benefits from them.
- Do not equate cooperation with obedience, consensus, or permanent dependence.
- Do not assume that a benevolent-seeming outcome legitimizes unaccountable authority.

## Related inquiry: selection versus enablement

The cross-disciplinary [Intelligence Without Omniscience](../research/intelligence-without-omniscience/README.md) working synthesis asks a complementary question: **what conditions allow beings and groups to develop and exercise cognitive capabilities?** This should not replace the ecology document's different question of **what behavior a selection environment rewards or preserves**. Distribution of resources, epistemic discoverability, accessibility, evidence recognition, and substantive freedom can affect effective capability without changing every individual's cognitive capacity. The focused [Epistemic Trust and Collective Verification](../research/intelligence-without-omniscience/epistemic-trust-and-collective-verification.md) inquiry examines how AI systems might reward agreeable but weakly supported explanations and what makes collective checking useful. A [2026 experimental study of AI sycophancy](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec8352) documents a task-bounded conflict between preferred affirmation and aspects of users' judgment; it does not establish any specific AI company's motives or a universal behavioral law. These connections are proposed translations, not independent proof of the ecology framework.

### An environment also selects which problems become *reported*

Selection pressures do not only influence which behaviors persist. They can also affect which events become *visible as evidence* to a decision system. An institution may count complaints and incorrectly treat their absence as absence of an underlying problem, even when risk of retaliation or lack of usable reporting channels suppresses testimony. A [2026 retail/food-service worker study](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/labor-standards-compliance-and-worker-complaints-new-data-and-insights) found substantial disparities between experienced labor violations and formal government complaints, with a role for retaliation concerns. That evidence is population- and context-specific; it does **not** establish a general mathematical law about all institutions.

The companion [epistemic knowledge-recognition research](../research/intelligence-without-omniscience/epistemic-injustice-and-knowledge-recognition.md#missing-knowledge-isnt-automatically-nonexistent-knowledge) distinguishes formal open/closed-world semantics, statistical missingness, and social pressures shaping disclosure. These are **different mechanisms**, not interchangeable metaphors. The existing ecology question becomes more precise: **what kinds of evidence does the system's institutional environment encourage, enable, suppress, or exclude from being recorded?** Do not mandate disclosure as a response; legitimate privacy, refusal, consent and safe alternatives remain essential.
