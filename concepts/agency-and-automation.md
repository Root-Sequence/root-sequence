**Evidence boundary:** The distinctions and examples below are normative design proposals. They are not empirical estimates of automatable labor or claims that particular systems are safe or effective.

## Working principle

> **Automate necessity. Preserve possibility.**

This is a provisional shorthand. A fuller version: remove unwanted, exhausting, dangerous, repetitive, or compulsory burdens when doing so helps people, while preserving meaningful ways to participate, delegate, refuse, inspect, contest, and change course.

The author's clarification is that automation and agency should be **balanced**. They want automation for friction, mundane work, and work they lack mental or physical energy to do, and envision much necessary paid labor becoming shared infrastructure so people can spend more of life on meaningful activity. This page develops that direction without treating one person's dislike of a task as a universal verdict on its meaning.

## Agency includes delegation

Doing a task by hand is one form of agency. Choosing that a trustworthy system handle it within intelligible boundaries can be another. Relevant capacities include:

- choosing a goal or declining the task;
- doing it oneself, sharing it, or delegating a bounded part;
- understanding what the system is allowed to do and what it actually did;
- pausing, correcting, challenging, revoking, or leaving an arrangement;
- retaining an accessible path when automation fails or is refused.

No person needs to master the machinery to deserve control. More explanations should be available to those who want them, without making technical fluency the price of a basic say. See [Legible Systems](legible-systems.md).

## Chosen activity and imposed burden

A task has no fixed amount of meaning independent of the person and context. Cleaning, cooking, calculation, organizing, caregiving, or construction may be wanted practice, shared care, paid obligation, hazardous exposure, or exhausting administration. The useful question is **who wants or must do it, under which conditions, with whose resources, and with what alternative?**

Distinguish:

- **Unwanted friction:** duplicated forms, needless waiting, inaccessible workflows, repetitive bookkeeping, arbitrary memory demands, preventable exposure to harm.
- **Chosen effort:** craft, learning, care, play, experimentation, or doing something by hand because one wants to.
- **Protective pause:** time for consent, review, deliberation, or safety before a consequential action.

Do not remove a protective pause merely because it costs time. Do not require people to perform a task manually as a test of worthiness or independence. Accessibility includes reducing demands on limited cognitive and physical energy.

## Modes, not a maturity ladder

The appropriate mode depends on the activity, affected parties, stakes, and ability to recover.

| Mode | Example question |
| --- | --- |
| Direct | Can I do this myself without compulsory mediation? |
| Assistive | Can the system reduce effort while I retain the action? |
| Suggestive | Can it offer a draft or recommendation I can ignore? |
| Delegated | Can I authorize a particular action with clear scope? |
| Standing arrangement | Can it handle routine cases under limits I can inspect, pause, and revise? |
| Background infrastructure | Can a shared necessary function be maintained reliably under legitimate collective governance? |

Initiation, observation, execution, authorization, review, and recovery are **separate dimensions**. An apparently reversible software edit may have already notified someone or changed a physical system. A highly automated service can be appropriate for one routine operation while a low-stakes personal choice stays direct. No mode is the endpoint for every activity.

## Delegation and affected parties

A person can delegate their own work; they cannot thereby authorize harm, surveillance, commitments, or decisions for everyone else. When automation affects a household, community, worker, bystander, or ecosystem, its authority must account for those relationships. A system should disclose who set the goal, who may change its rules, what data it uses, whose consent is relevant, who maintains it, and who can contest the result.

A bounded delegation should specify, as appropriate: **task and purpose; permitted information and tools; affected audience; duration; spending or physical limits; which exceptions interrupt; records; review and revocation; and what happens when the system is unavailable.** The burden of configuring all of this should not itself become another compulsory job; safe, intelligible defaults and proportionate controls matter.

Some decisions are inherently contested or irreversible. Technical ability to predict or execute does not settle legitimacy. See [Intelligence Ecology](intelligence-ecology.md) and [Idea Trail 9](../IDEA_TRAILS.md#trail-9--intelligence-automation-and-legitimate-authority).

## Dependency and recovery

Helpful automation can still create a brittle monopoly over a capability. Ask whether the person or community can see relevant state, export their work, substitute a provider or method, recover after failure, and continue at a simpler level. The fallback need only preserve the **essential capability** where full parity is impractical; it must not quietly discard consent, safety, or access. See [Resilience and Graceful Degradation](../systems/resilience-and-graceful-degradation.md).

## Beyond individual tasks

Automating more necessary labor does not by itself create free time or material security. Outcomes depend on who owns and governs the infrastructure, who receives the gains, who loses wages or bargaining power during transition, who supplies energy and materials, who does remaining care and maintenance, and whether essentials still depend on employment. These are questions for institutional design and evidence, not consequences guaranteed by a robot or model.

The aspiration is room for work, care, learning, rest, play, craft, research, community, and chosen difficulty without forcing people to sell most of their time to access essentials. Meaningful work remains possible; its availability and the distribution of burdens need deliberate design.

## Project translations to develop

- **Coherent Computing:** compare direct, assisted, and bounded automatic action across the same underlying activity; preserve authority, visible receipts, refusal, and feasible recovery. Existing requirements and CC-012 conceptual review decide specifics.
- **Community Infrastructure:** reduce coordination and administration burden while keeping Community Assistant optional, permission-aware, and unable to acquire collective authority from a participant's personal grant.
- **Coherent World simulation:** test different ownership and distribution rules after automation, including transition costs, maintenance, unequal effects, refusal, and chosen participation; treat scenarios as hypotheses, not forecasts.
- **Being Human(e):** collect lived edge cases of disability, fatigue, relief, dependence, humiliation, skill, care, and refusal before turning the principle into universal advice.

These are invitations for local work, not evidence that those changes are implemented.

## Open questions

1. Which forms of effort do people want to keep, and who gets to decide when preferences conflict?
2. When does help reduce a skill someone values, and when does it enable access that would otherwise be impossible?
3. What evidence shows an automation actually reduces total labor rather than hiding maintenance or shifting it to someone with less power?
4. Which actions need advance permission, which can be delegated under standing limits, and what makes a review genuinely meaningful?
5. What is a viable fallback when a task cannot safely be manual, and who pays to maintain it?
6. Who receives the productivity gains and material necessities during a transition away from compulsory work?
7. How do ecological costs and the possible interests of nonhuman intelligences alter the delegation boundary?
