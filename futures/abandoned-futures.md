# Abandoned Futures

> **We do not need to invent the future from scratch. It was here all along until it was abandoned.**

Some futures never existed beyond fantasy. Others existed partially: as prototypes, public programs, social practices, standards, institutions, technologies, infrastructure, research directions, or ordinary ways of living that were viable enough to leave evidence—but not powerful enough to survive the conditions around them.

This document studies those **abandoned futures**.

The central question is:

> **Was this future impossible—or merely unprofitable, politically blocked, poorly coordinated, enclosed, under-maintained, displaced by an incumbent, or abandoned before it could mature?**

---

## Working definition

An **abandoned future** is a materially or socially developed possibility that:

1. could have produced a meaningfully different trajectory;
2. advanced beyond pure speculation;
3. was interrupted, constrained, neglected, enclosed, or displaced;
4. remains recoverable through evidence, surviving practice, infrastructure, memory, or renewed development;
5. requires critical judgment rather than automatic revival.

The category can include:

- technologies;
- public services;
- infrastructure systems;
- institutional forms;
- legal frameworks;
- economic arrangements;
- standards and protocols;
- repair and maintenance cultures;
- social practices;
- ecological methods;
- forms of governance;
- unrealized combinations of existing systems.

---

## Ways a future is abandoned

### Cancelled

A project is deliberately ended through a political, administrative, corporate, or financial decision.

### Starved

The project technically continues but loses staffing, maintenance, funding, access, institutional support, or time until failure becomes predictable.

### Enclosed

A shared or public capability is transformed into private property, proprietary infrastructure, a subscription, a platform dependency, or a controlled market.

### Standardized out

A potentially useful path loses because another standard becomes dominant, even when the dominant path is not universally better.

### Displaced by incumbency

Existing firms, infrastructures, land uses, professional norms, or political coalitions make alternatives appear impractical by controlling the environment in which practicality is judged.

### Made artificially expensive

Policy, procurement, financing, liability, intellectual property, zoning, licensing, or fragmented responsibility imposes costs that are treated as intrinsic to the idea.

### Made illegible

The value exists but is difficult to price, measure, patent, own, advertise, audit, or attribute to one institution.

### Piloted without a path to permanence

A temporary experiment demonstrates possibility but is never given durable governance, maintenance, funding, or authority.

### Abandoned through coordination failure

The pieces exist, but no institution can connect them, absorb transition costs, or protect early participants.

### Forgotten

Institutional memory disappears. Staff leave. Documentation decays. A later generation mistakes absence of awareness for absence of prior work.

### Extracted and discarded

An institution captures the profitable component while abandoning the public, ecological, social, or infrastructural whole that made it valuable.

### Rejected for good reason

Some futures should remain abandoned because they were harmful, exclusionary, brittle, authoritarian, ecologically destructive, technically unsound, or dependent on hidden exploitation.

The project must preserve this category. **Abandoned does not mean worthy.**

## The profitability filter

Modern technical culture often confuses three questions:

1. Can this be built?
2. Would this improve life?
3. Can an owner reliably extract increasing returns from it?

A negative answer to the third question can erase the first two from public imagination.

Useful systems may be disfavored because they are:

- durable rather than repeatedly sold;
- repairable rather than replaceable;
- public rather than proprietary;
- interoperable rather than enclosed;
- preventive rather than billable after failure;
- distributed rather than centrally owned;
- sufficient rather than growth-maximizing;
- labor-saving for workers rather than labor-extracting for owners;
- accessible without producing a high-value customer;
- ecologically restorative without creating a tradable asset;
- successful in ways that eliminate the market that funded them.

This does not mean profit explains every abandonment. It means profitability is often treated as a measure of feasibility when it is only one institutional selection mechanism.

---

## The shareholder veto

A future may be technically possible, socially useful, publicly desired, and institutionally legible—then disappear because the actors with veto power do not capture enough of its benefit.

This creates a recurring pattern:

```text
shared benefit
+ diffuse beneficiaries
+ concentrated transition cost
+ concentrated veto power
+ weak public coordination
= viable future abandoned
```

The joke that “the future was possible until a shareholder voted against it” is funny because it compresses a real systems problem: those who can stop a transition are often easier to organize than those who would benefit from it.

---

## Not nostalgia

Abandoned-future work is not an attempt to recreate an imagined golden age.

A recovered future may require:

- new accessibility standards;
- ecological limits;
- different ownership;
- repair of historical exclusion;
- modern safety and privacy protections;
- changed materials or energy sources;
- decolonization;
- public or commons governance;
- integration with technologies that did not previously exist;
- refusal of the original project's harmful assumptions.

The goal is not restoration to an untouched earlier form.

> **Recover the possibility, not the hierarchy that once contained it.**

---

## Not technological determinism

Technology does not carry one inevitable social order.

The same underlying capability can be organized as:

- public infrastructure;
- a cooperative;
- a commons;
- a local utility;
- a subscription platform;
- a surveillance system;
- a luxury product;
- a workplace mandate;
- an accessible public good;
- an extractive dependency.

Recovering a technology without changing its ownership, governance, maintenance, and purpose may reproduce the same failure under a futuristic surface.

## Institutional archaeology

Abandoned futures leave remains:

- empty facilities;
- buried conduit;
- unused rail alignments;
- discontinued hardware;
- source code;
- patents and standards;
- procurement records;
- manuals;
- pilot evaluations;
- employee knowledge;
- community memory;
- forgotten websites;
- datasets;
- warehouses;
- automated processes still running after their institution disappears;
- ordinary objects whose original system is no longer visible.

Studying those remains is **institutional archaeology**.

The work asks:

- What did this system do?
- Who understood it?
- Who depended on it?
- Why did it end?
- What still functions?
- What claims and harms remain attached to it?
- Who has authority to recover, reuse, publish, or transform it?
- What labor would make it safe?

Recoverability is not permission. Ruins can still contain private data, ecological hazards, community claims, cultural meaning, labor history, and unresolved responsibility.

---

## The maintenance test

Many imagined futures are launch fantasies. They explain how something begins but not how it remains usable.

Before calling a future viable, ask:

- Who inspects it?
- Who cleans it?
- Who updates it?
- Who repairs it?
- Who documents changes?
- Who trains successors?
- Where do replacement parts come from?
- What happens when the vendor disappears?
- How does it fail?
- Who receives warnings?
- Who bears degraded service first?
- Can local people understand and alter it?
- Is maintenance respected, funded, and visible?

A future that requires invisible sacrifice is not coherent merely because its interface looks advanced.

## Candidate domains

This framework can investigate abandoned or suppressed possibilities in:

- public transportation and human-scale mobility;
- rail, streetcar, bicycle, walking, and mixed-mode networks;
- public and cooperative housing;
- district energy and shared utilities;
- repairable and modular electronics;
- open protocols and interoperable software;
- public-interest computing;
- community networks;
- durable appliances and tools;
- preventive healthcare and public health infrastructure;
- universal provision;
- libraries and knowledge commons;
- local manufacturing and fabrication;
- ecological restoration;
- passive design and climate-adapted architecture;
- shorter work time and labor-saving automation;
- cooperative and municipal ownership;
- mutual aid and community care;
- alternative educational institutions;
- public space and common land;
- archives designed around consent and provenance.

This is a research agenda, not a claim that every item contains one obvious lost solution.

## Coherent World principle

The transformed world should not look as though one intelligence invented a perfect civilization from blank space.

It should contain:

- revived public systems;
- repaired infrastructure;
- recombined old and new technologies;
- human projects that finally received enough protection and coordination to endure;
- obsolete objects kept through love, usefulness, habit, or historical value;
- local variations;
- systems that were deliberately refused;
- visible maintenance;
- traces of the abandoned world;
- disagreements over what deserves recovery.

Auryn's power may change which futures can survive. It must not erase the people who imagined, built, maintained, defended, and remembered them.

---

## Research rule

Do not begin with the conclusion that capitalism killed a perfect invention.

Begin with evidence:

1. reconstruct what actually existed;
2. distinguish promise from demonstrated capacity;
3. identify technical and social limitations;
4. map institutions and interests;
5. examine competing explanations;
6. identify harms and excluded groups;
7. determine what, if anything, remains recoverable;
8. state uncertainty.

The motif is strongest when the history is more complicated than the slogan.

> **The future is not a destination waiting ahead. It is a field of possibilities continually built, blocked, forgotten, recovered, and transformed.**
