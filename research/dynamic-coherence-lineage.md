# Adaptive continuity / dynamic coherence — intellectual lineage and prior art

**Document role:** Targeted related-work and intellectual-lineage note  
**Status:** AI-assisted exploratory synthesis for author review, 2026-09-24  
**Scope:** Identifies established concepts that overlap pieces of the Root Sequence adaptive-continuity / dynamic-coherence synthesis. It does not establish novelty, shared causal mechanism, or a complete literature review.  
**Canonical concept:** [Dynamic Coherence and Adaptive Continuity](../concepts/dynamic-coherence.md)

## Why this note exists

The September 24 synthesis initially felt unusually generative because it connected:

- state change and changes in how later change happens;
- path dependence;
- agency and future possibility;
- feedback and revision;
- continuity through transformation;
- emergence and downward constraint;
- resilience and adaptation;
- reflexivity;
- power over constraints;
- AI and human-system boundaries.

That does **not** imply the component ideas are new.

The right research question is:

> Which pieces have already been developed more precisely elsewhere, and what—if anything—is distinctive about how Root Sequence connects them?

The answer after this first pass is:

> **Many of the core dynamics have deep predecessors. The candidate contribution, if any, is more likely an integrative grammar linking those dynamics to agency, power, continuity, legitimacy, embodiment, and cross-project design.**

That hypothesis still needs comparison.

# 1. Ashby: state change versus parameter change

One of the strongest prior-art findings is W. Ross Ashby's distinction between:

- a change in a system variable/state; and
- a change in a parameter that changes the field/dynamics governing behavior.

In *Feedback, Adaptation and Stability* (1960), Ashby explicitly emphasizes that distinguishing a change of variable from a change of parameter separates changes that are **behavior** from changes **from one behavior to another**.

Sources:

- W. Ross Ashby, "Feedback, Adaptation and Stability" (1960): https://panarchy.org/ashby/adaptation.1960.html
- Related book: *Design for a Brain: The Origin of Adaptive Behaviour*, especially the sections on parameters and ultrastability.

## Relation to Root Sequence

This is a direct conceptual ancestor of the draft distinction between current state and transition dynamics.

Root Sequence should therefore **not claim novelty** for the basic distinction between current-state change and change in transition dynamics.

What remains potentially distinctive is how the distinction is connected to:

- agency;
- future reachable possibility;
- power over revision;
- identity/continuity;
- social and institutional constraints;
- legitimacy and externalized costs.

---

# 2. Ashby: ultrastability and second-order adaptation

Ashby's **ultrastable system** is an early formal/mechanical account of a system changing parameters when ordinary dynamics drive essential variables toward unacceptable or critical states.

The conceptual pattern is:

    ordinary dynamics fail to keep essential variables viable
            ↓
    parameter / field changes
            ↓
    new behavior regime
            ↓
    retain regimes that avoid critical states

This is much closer to our "change how the system changes" insight than a simple thermostat metaphor.

Sources:

- W. Ross Ashby, *Design for a Brain*, chapter sequence on parameters and the ultrastable system: https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-94-015-1320-3
- Ashby, "Feedback, Adaptation and Stability" (1960): https://panarchy.org/ashby/adaptation.1960.html
- W. Ross Ashby Digital Archive: https://www.ashby.info/archive.html

## Boundary

Ultrastability is **not**:

- a general theory of human flourishing;
- proof of consciousness;
- a political theory of agency;
- a universal model for institutions;
- evidence that every adaptive process optimizes the same viability criterion.

Use it as a strong cybernetic predecessor for adaptive rule/parameter change.

# 3. Requisite variety: response capacity versus disturbance variety

Ashby's Law of Requisite Variety places a formal bound on regulation: a regulator needs sufficient variety or information capacity relative to the disturbances it must regulate.

Primary source:

- W. Ross Ashby, *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956), especially the Requisite Variety chapters: https://ashby.info/Ashby-Introduction-to-Cybernetics.pdf

## Relation to Root Sequence

This gives an important ancestor for questions such as:

- Does the system have enough distinct responses for the disturbances it can encounter?
- Has optimization narrowed the response repertoire?
- Does one bottleneck collapse many nominal options into one effective response?
- Does a system preserve enough diversity to remain adaptable?

But requisite variety is **not equivalent to agency**.

A human agency map also involves:

- knowledge;
- perception;
- material feasibility;
- authorization;
- consent;
- social and institutional power;
- values;
- relationships.

So:

> **Requisite variety may help formalize response diversity; it does not replace the agency-envelope question.**

# 4. Conant & Ashby: the Good Regulator theorem

Conant and Ashby's 1970 Good Regulator theorem argues, under specific formal assumptions, that a maximally successful and simple regulator must embody a model of the system it regulates.

Source:

- Roger C. Conant and W. Ross Ashby, "Every Good Regulator of a System Must Be a Model of That System," *International Journal of Systems Science* 1(2), 1970, 89–97. DOI: 10.1080/00207727008920220
- Accessible scan: https://firstlaw.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/10/goodregulator.pdf

## Relation to Root Sequence

This is an important predecessor for the recurring loop:

> observe → model → imagine → intervene → consequences → learn → adapt

It supports taking **model quality** seriously in regulation/control.

It does not establish:

- that one centralized model is always best;
- that prediction grants legitimate authority;
- that a regulator's model is consciously experienced;
- that descriptive accuracy decides values or goals.

The theorem belongs in the formal lineage, not as moral authorization.

# 5. Argyris and Schön: single-loop versus double-loop learning

Organizational-learning theory distinguishes:

### Single-loop learning

Change action strategies while leaving governing variables/assumptions substantially intact.

### Double-loop learning

Question and revise the governing variables themselves.

This is extremely close to the Root Sequence distinction between:

- acting within the current transition structure; and
- changing the structure, rules, or criteria that generate later action.

Sources:

- Chris Argyris, "Double Loop Learning in Organizations," *Harvard Business Review* (1977): https://hbr.org/1977/09/double-loop-learning-in-organizations
- Accessible overview of the governing-variable distinction: https://actiondesign.com/resources/readings/double-loop-learning

## Relation to Root Sequence

For organizations and institutions, **double-loop learning may be a more established and precise term** than inventing generic transition-function revision language.

Root Sequence's contribution should be to route to this literature when organizational governing assumptions are the actual mechanism.

# 6. Resilience engineering: respond, monitor, learn, anticipate

Erik Hollnagel's resilience-engineering work emphasizes four systemic potentials:

- respond;
- monitor;
- learn;
- anticipate.

Sources:

- Erik Hollnagel, overview of resilience engineering and systemic potentials: https://erikhollnagel.com/ideas/resilience-engineering-2004
- Resilience Analysis Grid overview: https://resilienceengineeringinstitute.org/resilience-analysis-grid/

A particularly relevant extension is Hollnagel's description of systems that recursively consider how their **own changes alter the world and later conditions**.

## Relation to Root Sequence

This overlaps strongly with:

- feedback quality;
- model revision;
- anticipatory design;
- intervention consequences;
- reflexivity.

This is another reason Dynamic Coherence should **not** be marketed as though no mature field already studies adaptive socio-technical performance.

# 7. Woods: graceful extensibility and sustained adaptability

David Woods distinguishes several resilience ideas, including:

- graceful extensibility;
- sustained adaptability.

Sustained adaptability is especially relevant because it concerns the capacity to **continue adapting as environments, demands, stakeholders, contexts, and constraints continue changing**.

Sources:

- David D. Woods, "The theory of graceful extensibility: basic rules that govern adaptive systems," *Environment Systems and Decisions* 38 (2018). DOI: 10.1007/s10669-018-9708-3
- Resilience Engineering Association publication overview: https://www.resilience-engineering-association.org/resources/publications/

## Why this matters

This is extremely close to our proposed design concern:

> preserve the capacity for future adaptation.

Woods also foregrounds finite adaptive capacity and brittleness near capacity boundaries.

That suggests Root Sequence should compare its terms against:

- graceful extensibility;
- sustained adaptability;
- adaptive capacity;
- brittleness.

before claiming a distinct resilience construct.

# 8. Adaptive management: interventions as experiments

Adaptive management in ecology/resource management treats policies as hypotheses and management actions as opportunities to learn.

A common pattern is:

    explicit model / hypothesis
            ↓
    bounded intervention
            ↓
    monitor
            ↓
    learn
            ↓
    revise management

Sources / lineage:

- Walters and Holling's adaptive-management work;
- overview of adaptive management as policies-as-hypotheses / actions-as-experiments: https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/adaptive-management
- historical appraisal: https://ecologyandsociety.net/vol3/iss2/art3/

## Relation to Root Sequence

This is a direct predecessor of the proposed:

> generate → interrogate → simulate → experience → learn

and Coherent World's:

> observe → model → imagine → intervene → consequences → learn → adapt.

Again, the loop is not novel.

What may be distinctive is connecting adaptive experimentation with:

- agency;
- consent;
- reversibility;
- affected-party authority;
- future option structure;
- fictional/simulation exploration.

# 9. Adaptive governance

Adaptive-governance research examines how networks of individuals, organizations, agencies, and institutions support reorganization and learning in social-ecological systems under change.

Source:

- Carl Folke, Thomas Hahn, Per Olsson, Jon Norberg, "Adaptive Governance of Social-Ecological Systems," *Annual Review of Environment and Resources* 30 (2005), 441–473. DOI: 10.1146/annurev.energy.30.050504.144511

## Relation to Root Sequence

This is especially relevant to:

- Community Infrastructure;
- collective decision architecture;
- cross-scale governance;
- institutional flexibility;
- local knowledge;
- crisis/reorganization.

Root Sequence should route institutional claims here instead of reinventing them through abstract coherence vocabulary.

# 10. Path dependence and lock-in

Path dependence is a large family of literatures rather than one mechanism.

Paul Pierson's influential institutional account emphasizes increasing returns, timing/sequence, and how earlier choices can make later reversal increasingly difficult.

Source:

- Paul Pierson, "Increasing Returns, Path Dependence, and the Study of Politics," *American Political Science Review* 94(2), 2000, 251–267. DOI: 10.2307/2586011

## Relation to Root Sequence

The phrase:

> **history becomes part of the structure generating the future**

is a useful cross-project summary.

But causal claims should name the mechanism:

- increasing returns;
- switching costs;
- learning effects;
- coordination effects;
- infrastructure;
- law;
- habit;
- physiological learning;
- network topology;
- resource accumulation;
- institutional precedent.

"Path dependence" must not become a magic word for "the past matters."

# 11. Second-order cybernetics and the observer

Second-order cybernetics turns cybernetic analysis back onto the observing or controlling system.

A classic distinction associated with Heinz von Foerster is:

- first-order cybernetics: cybernetics of **observed systems**;
- second-order cybernetics: cybernetics of **observing systems**.

Related sources:

- historical introduction: https://doi.org/10.1108/03684920410556007
- system/observer relations: https://doi.org/10.1111/1468-5914.00160

## Relation to Root Sequence

This is highly relevant to the insight that:

- models are produced from boundaries and perspectives;
- measurements can change behavior;
- predictions and classifications can become interventions;
- the analyst/designer is not automatically outside the system;
- system descriptions carry responsibility and power.

Root Sequence's reflexivity work should engage this lineage directly.

# 12. Soft systems and critical systems: boundaries, worldviews, power, legitimacy

The strongest prior-art correction to the **power / boundary / legitimacy** side of Root Sequence comes from Soft Systems Methodology (SSM), Critical Systems Heuristics (CSH), and Critical Systems Thinking (CST).

These traditions already reject the idea that a systems analyst can simply draw a neutral boundary, optimize the enclosed system, and call the result rational.

## Soft Systems Methodology

Checkland's Soft Systems Methodology treats human problem situations as involving multiple legitimate descriptions and worldviews rather than one objectively given problem statement.

Its well-known CATWOE mnemonic asks analysts to make explicit:

- **Customers** — people who benefit or suffer from the transformation;
- **Actors** — those carrying out the activities;
- **Transformation** — what change is being described;
- **Weltanschauung / worldview** — the perspective that makes the transformation meaningful;
- **Owner** — who has power to stop or alter the system;
- **Environmental constraints** — conditions treated as outside local control.

That is very close to Root Sequence's repeated insistence that:

- system boundaries are choices;
- purposes are not neutral;
- several actors can experience the same intervention differently;
- authority over the transformation must be named;
- environmental constraints cannot be wished away.

SSM is therefore an important predecessor whenever Root Sequence asks whose model of the situation is being used.

Sources / entry points:

- Peter Checkland's SSM tradition and CATWOE;
- overview of CATWOE and perspective-explication: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/225982790_Basic_principles_of_SSM_modeling_An_examination_of_CATWOE_from_a_soft_perspective

## Critical Systems Heuristics

Werner Ulrich's Critical Systems Heuristics makes **boundary critique** explicit.

Its twelve boundary questions are organized around four sources of influence:

- **motivation** — whose interests/purposes define improvement;
- **control / power** — who can decide and control resources;
- **knowledge** — whose expertise and experience count;
- **legitimacy** — how the interests of people affected but not involved are represented.

CSH distinguishes people who are **involved** in a system or intervention from people who are **affected** without controlling it.

This is a very close predecessor to Root Sequence questions such as:

- Who defined the boundary?
- Who benefits?
- Who bears costs outside the local objective?
- Who can revise the arrangement?
- Whose knowledge is treated as evidence?
- Who is affected without a decision right?
- What makes authority legitimate?

Sources:

- Werner Ulrich and Martin Reynolds, "Critical Systems Heuristics" chapter: https://wulrich.com/downloads/ulrich_reynolds_2010_prepub.pdf
- Werner Ulrich, "Critical Systems Heuristics" overview: https://wulrich.com/downloads/ulrich_2002b.pdf

## Critical Systems Thinking

Critical Systems Thinking developed partly from dissatisfaction with any one systems methodology being treated as universally adequate.

Different formulations identify commitments including:

- critical awareness;
- social awareness;
- methodological and theoretical pluralism / complementarism;
- human emancipation.

Gerald Midgley's compressed formulation emphasizes:

- **critical awareness** — question taken-for-granted assumptions and the conditions producing them;
- **emancipation** — define improvement locally and temporarily while examining power;
- **methodological pluralism** — use methods according to their strengths and limits rather than forcing every problem through one framework.

This is especially important after the T-003 editorial pilots.

Those pilots independently pushed Root Sequence toward:

> **use competent domain methods first; use Dynamic Coherence as a routing/synthesis grammar when it connects consequential questions those methods leave separated.**

That methodological humility is not novel.

It strongly resembles the critical-systems commitment to methodological pluralism.

Sources:

- Michael C. Jackson, "The origins and nature of critical systems thinking," *Systemic Practice and Action Research* 4 (1991), 131–149. DOI: 10.1007/BF01068246
- Michael C. Jackson, "Five Commitments of Critical Systems Thinking" (1991).
- Gerald Midgley, "What is this thing called CST?" in *Critical Systems Thinking* (1996), DOI: 10.1007/978-0-585-34651-9_1

## Implication for Root Sequence

The power/legitimacy/boundary side of the September 24 synthesis is **not an unexplored territory**.

Root Sequence should explicitly learn from CSH/CST rather than recreating boundary critique under a coherence vocabulary.

A candidate local contribution is not:

> "systems thinking should consider power."

That is old and well-developed.

A more defensible question is:

> **Can Root Sequence make critical boundary/power analysis easy to carry between technical design, AI, embodiment/consciousness research, speculative simulation, community infrastructure, and everyday humane practice without flattening each field's own methods?**

That remains open.

# 13. Stafford Beer: viability, autonomy, cohesion, and future-facing adaptation

Stafford Beer's Viable System Model (VSM) is another important organizational-cybernetics predecessor.

The VSM describes recursive organizational functions needed for viability and emphasizes a relationship between **local autonomy and system-level cohesion**.

Its future/environment-facing function, commonly described as System 4, is concerned with environmental change and adaptation; System 5 is associated with identity/policy and balancing present operations against future adaptation.

This makes the VSM relevant to Root Sequence questions about:

- organizational continuity;
- identity while changing;
- distributed/local autonomy;
- environmental sensing;
- future adaptation;
- coordination without complete centralization.

A sustainability-oriented review of Beer specifically emphasizes autonomy and cohesion in rapidly changing environments.

Source:

- Espinosa, Harnden, and Walker, "A complexity approach to sustainability – Stafford Beer revisited," *European Journal of Operational Research* 187 (2008), 636–651. DOI: 10.1016/j.ejor.2007.03.023

## Evidence caution

The VSM makes a **strong theory claim** about preconditions for organizational viability.

Later empirical work has attempted to test that theory and reports supportive results, while practical-application literature also identifies difficulties in applying the model.

That is exactly the kind of situation Root Sequence should treat carefully:

- theory;
- case applications;
- empirical tests;
- implementation difficulty;

are different evidence layers.

Sources:

- Schwaninger and Scheef, "A Test of the Viable System Model: Theoretical Claim vs. Empirical Evidence," *Cybernetics and Systems* 47(7) (2016), 544–569. DOI: 10.1080/01969722.2016.1209375
- practical-application critique: DOI 10.1108/K-02-2017-0069

The VSM should be studied as a serious predecessor, not adopted wholesale as Root Sequence architecture.

# 14. Prior use of the phrase “dynamic coherence”

The phrase **dynamic coherence** is not original to Root Sequence and is not unambiguous.

At least two materially different existing uses are easy to identify.

## Lane & Oliva (1998)

A systems-methodology paper uses "dynamic coherence" for consistency between intuitive expectations about behavior and behavior deduced from a causal/system-dynamics model.

- David C. Lane and Rogelio Oliva, "The greater whole: Towards a synthesis of system dynamics and soft systems methodology," *European Journal of Operational Research* 107(1), 1998, 214–235. DOI: 10.1016/S0377-2217(97)00205-1

This use is related to model consistency, but it is **not the same construct** as Root Sequence's temporal/adaptive synthesis.

## Dynamic Coherence Window (2026)

A 2026 AI paper uses "Dynamic Coherence Window" for a specific multi-scale structural-coherence measurement framework for AI outputs.

- Christian St-Louis, "Dynamic coherence windows: a multi-scale structural coherence measurement framework for AI systems," *Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence* 9 (2026), 1836120. DOI: 10.3389/frai.2026.1836120

Again, this is a different framework.

## Naming implication

Root Sequence must **not** claim:

- coinage of "dynamic coherence";
- exclusive use of the term;
- identity with these existing frameworks.

Possible responses:

1. keep **Dynamic Coherence** as clearly qualified internal project language;
2. prefer **Adaptive Continuity** for the cross-project concept;
3. use **dynamic-coherence grammar** only as a descriptive label;
4. rename after related-work review.

No naming decision is made by this note.

# 16. What looks genuinely old versus potentially distinctive

## Strong predecessors / not novel as components

- state change vs parameter/rule change;
- adaptive regulation;
- response variety;
- regulator models;
- double-loop learning;
- resilience as respond/monitor/learn/anticipate;
- sustained adaptability;
- path dependence;
- adaptive management;
- observer/reflexivity problems;
- worldview-explication and soft systems methodology;
- boundary critique around motivation, power, knowledge, and legitimacy;
- critical systems methodological pluralism;
- organizational viability/autonomy/cohesion models.

## Candidate Root Sequence synthesis

Potentially distinctive—not yet demonstrated as novel—is the particular project-level integration and routing of these traditions into:

- **agency:** which meaningful possibilities remain practically reachable;
- **meta-agency:** who can shape future possibility and decision structure;
- **power:** who controls constraints, interfaces, resources, rules, and revision;
- **identity/continuity:** what persists through transformation;
- **embodiment:** the physical organism as part of the process;
- **legitimacy:** capability does not imply authority;
- **externalized effects:** local adaptation can move costs elsewhere;
- **cross-scale effects:** one level's stability can create another level's fragility;
- **epistemic humility:** the framework must route to domain methods rather than totalize them.

That is a hypothesis about the value of the synthesis.

It is not yet a novelty finding.

# Working conclusion

The discovery of strong prior art does not make the September 24 synthesis less interesting.

It changes what is interesting about it.

The emerging question is no longer:

> **Did Root Sequence discover a new law of adaptive systems?**

The better question is:

> **Can Root Sequence build a legible, humane, cross-domain grammar that connects established insights about adaptation and regulation to agency, power, identity, continuity, and legitimacy—without flattening their differences?**

That is both more modest and, potentially, more useful.
