> More information can improve a decision without changing the assumptions that determine which decisions are available. When those assumptions are wrong or exclusionary, what would it take for a system to revise them—and who is authorized to do so?

## 1. Why this is distinct from fact sharing

Our earlier inquiry decomposed a path from observed information through disclosure, evidence evaluation, incorporation into a model, institutional uptake, practical means, and legitimate authorization.

A different problem remains after an institution has received, verified, and even *accepted* the evidence:

- Its objective might still be inappropriate for the actual situation.
- Its procedures might forbid proposing alternative questions.
- It may be allowed to adjust an action without challenging underlying decision rules.
- An announced reform may not govern actual decisions.
- The organization may change a goal legitimately but lack resources, accountability, or a feasible intervention.
- A goal may be **worth preserving**, even when one intervention goes badly.

Thus a richer model does not by itself establish that an organization can or should alter its goals. This is an investigation into **the operating conditions and authorization of revision**.

## 2. Existing native field: Argyris and Schön's organizational learning

Chris Argyris and Donald Schön developed **single-loop** and **double-loop** learning; see Argyris's [1977 *Harvard Business Review* article](https://hbr.org/1977/09/double-loop-learning-in-organizations) and their 1978 book *Organizational Learning: A Theory of Action Perspective*.

**Single-loop:** a discrepancy between intended and observed consequences is addressed by revising *action strategies* while the underlying values, assumptions, and governing variables remain unchanged.

**Double-loop:** the underlying **governing variables** are critically examined and revised, *and* actual subsequent behavior or organizational practice changes accordingly.

**Espoused theory versus theory-in-use:** what an institution says it values may differ from the rules one can infer from its decisions and behavior. An updated mission statement or a workshop endorsement is **not by itself evidence** of transformed governing practice.

A [2023 systematic review of 128 studies](https://doi.org/10.1111/emre.12615) by Auqui-Caceres and Furlan identifies significant conceptual and measurement confusion. It emphasizes detecting **both changed governing assumptions and changed actual action**, cautions against inferring learning solely from self-report, and identifies defensive routines and context as major barriers. This is a **research review**, not evidence that the requested change will occur simply because an organization schedules reflection sessions.

**Critical caution:** the goals themselves can be appropriate while execution is flawed. Correcting the action without reopening the goal may be precisely the right move. "Higher loops" are not morally or practically superior by definition.

## 3. Another important distinction: whose boundary and whose purpose?

Changing a goal within an organization is not the same as enabling affected people to change **who defines the problem, who is included, or what counts as success**.

Three complementary bodies of work should remain distinct:

### Soft Systems Methodology (SSM)

The [University of Cambridge's overview](https://www.ifm.eng.cam.ac.uk/research/dstools/soft-systems-methodology/) attributes SSM to Peter Checkland and collaborators. SSM addresses **problematic situations**, different worldviews and proposed purposeful activities, instead of assuming the problem and its objective are simply given. Its CATWOE prompts consideration of clients/customers, actors, transformation, worldview, owners, and environmental constraints.

**Research translation:** before optimizing a municipal metric, investigate what *different participants think the situation actually is*. Those accounts can reveal different problems; they need not be equally accurate about every technical claim.

### Critical Systems Heuristics (CSH)

Werner Ulrich's [own explanation of boundary critique](https://i2insights.org/2022/05/24/critical-systems-heuristics/) emphasizes judgments determining what a system includes, whose interests it serves, and who can participate. CSH contrasts what *is* with what *ought to be*: intended beneficiaries, purpose, decision power, resources, knowledge and legitimacy.

**Research translation:** changing an operational metric might still leave the boundary of whose experiences count unchanged. A credible system-level analysis should make both **the boundary and the power to redraw it** contestable.

### Second-order cybernetics

Second-order cybernetics, associated with Heinz von Foerster and others, foregrounds the **observer as part of the systems being observed**. This is an epistemological and methodological orientation, not a claim that every actor is conscious or that every organization with feedback is a double-loop learner. [Review of observer-inclusive perspectives, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11711560/).

**Research translation:** the questionnaire, metrics, interface, and research intervention may change the behavior they are intended to measure. A "transparent" system's observability and categories are themselves causal features, not neutral windows.

### Relationship, not synonymy

| Native approach | Investigates primarily | What it doesn't establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Single-/double-loop learning | Are action strategies or actual governing variables revised? | That revised goals are legitimate or beneficial for everyone |
| SSM | How is the problematic situation framed from different worldviews? | Which fact claims are true simply because someone holds a viewpoint |
| CSH | Whose interests, expertise, authority and measures define the system boundary? | A single correct governance arrangement for every institution |
| Second-order cybernetics | How do observers, categories and observation participate in feedback? | That a specific institution learns, or possesses self-awareness |
| Capability approach | What resources and conversion conditions enable substantive opportunities? | Automatic political permission to perform a technically possible act |
| Coherent World | What do declared fictional mechanisms imply when recombined? | Real-world causal evidence or a canonical societal blueprint |

## 4. Resist the alluring "triple-loop" shortcut

Several authors describe **triple-loop learning** as learning about how to learn, questioning deeper frames, or reshaping learning institutions. But the term is contested.

- [Tosey, Visser & Saunders (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1177/1350507611426239) show divergent conceptual lineages and weak theoretical/empirical grounding in many uses, and note that the term is **not from Argyris and Schön's published work**.
- [Kwon, Kang & Nicolaides (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1108/TLO-01-2024-0030) systematically reviewed 23 empirical studies. They find themes concerning participative leadership, power relations, learning cultures, and collective knowledge creation, but also note persistent definitional ambiguity.

It could be useful to investigate *who can change the rules governing revision itself*, but Root Sequence should **describe that precise mechanism** instead of automatically naming a new "third loop" or ranking layers as necessarily better.

This is where local **meta-agency** may provide a useful question about possible future action, while the empirical explanatory work remains with native institutional, organizational, and political research.

## 5. A minimal operating distinction

An inquiry might distinguish:

1. **Outcome monitoring:** which observations signal a mismatch? What did we leave unmeasured?
2. **Strategy revision:** can the institution change a tactic while keeping the same governing target?
3. **Governing-assumption revision:** can it question, approve and actually enact changes to the rules/values/metric guiding decisions?
4. **Problem-boundary revision:** can differently situated people contest the categories and scope of what is being optimized?
5. **Authorization:** who has legitimate authority to modify practices, goals, and the revision procedure? What constraints or rights apply?
6. **Material and practical conversion:** what resources, maintenance, time, staffing, knowledge and infrastructure can make the revised action feasible?
7. **Observation of actual use:** did everyday institutional behavior change, or only the document describing it?
8. **Recursive effects:** did the response widen future people's practical capacity to challenge and change the arrangement, or create new dependence?

These are **diagnostic axes**, not a universal sequence or a new numerical coherence score. Different cases legitimately omit, reorder, or dispute stages.

## 6. Three illustrative transit-policy counterfactuals

All facts, goals, roles and outcomes below are **invented**, not a description of a real transit agency.

**Initial institutional target:** maximize a declared throughput indicator. **Accepted new observation:** a group with accessibility and care-timing needs remains unserved despite improvements to aggregate throughput.

- **Case A: single-loop:** Increase frequency to improve the existing throughput measure, without revising how "successful service" is defined. This can be a valid strategy for the declared measure; it doesn't prove the access problem was addressed.
- **Case B: apparent double-loop:** The agency announces that all trips should be accessible but its binding purchasing rules, street interventions, available options, and accountability processes remain unchanged. This is **espoused reform**, not adequate evidence of revised theory-in-use.
- **Case C: authorized enacted revision:** The relevant authorized decision process revises its operative rule to require continuous accessible journeys in addition to defined reliability outcomes. It changes actual plans and resource allocations, and then observes consequences. Under Argyris/Schön this is a better *candidate* for double-loop learning, though judging its success also requires evidence of enacted behavior, effects, and the rule's legitimacy.

**Negative cases:**
- A materially false objection may be rejected with reasons, with no rule change warranted.
- A real concern may be recognized while no action is feasible; that is a resource or authority limitation, not necessarily institutional ignorance.
- A changed goal may disadvantage other affected people and require renewed deliberation, repair, or reversal.
- A more participatory process can create undue unpaid review and care burdens, exclusion, capture, or indecision.
- A legitimate goal revision may be correctly rejected because the original goal was justified, or because the proposal fails applicable rights and authorization constraints.

The [third Coherent World paper probe](https://github.com/Root-Sequence/coherent-world/blob/research/intelligence-without-omniscience-2026-10/simulation/scenarios/distributed-intelligence/MECHANISM-PROBE-003.md) already separates information receipt, uptake, means and legitimate permission. A follow-up [goal-revision protocol](https://github.com/Root-Sequence/coherent-world/blob/research/intelligence-without-omniscience-2026-10/simulation/scenarios/distributed-intelligence/MECHANISM-PROBE-005.md) can isolate strategy, governing rule, problem framing and observed implementation, without introducing an autonomous institutional AI.

## 7. What would actually count as evidence?

A serious empirical case would need more than self-reported reflection:
- The exact **governing variable before** the observed challenge, recovered from enforceable rules and actual choices.
- The **evidence or criticism received**, including original wording, time, credibility, possible privacy constraints and whether it was relevant.
- The **decision rule governing revision**, whose authorization it depended on, and which affected people could challenge it.
- A documented, legitimate **change to the governing variable**—not merely new public-facing language.
- A changed **observed action** linked to that revision, with checks for alternative causes.
- The **outcome and consequences**, including who gained and lost capability, whose labor increased, and whether the goal revision was reversible.
- Independent or reasonably appropriate audit of the before/after behavior, rather than sole reliance on the organization's own account.

Counterfactual design: hold the factual observation constant while varying (a) permission to challenge a goal; (b) authorized process; (c) available material resources; (d) ability to enact a revised rule. Include a case with an originally valid goal and an inaccurate objection, so the experiment doesn't reward rule changes simply for being changes.

That research design cannot magically adjudicate ethical or political disagreements. A model can show that a rule changed; the *right* to have changed it requires independent institutional evaluation.

## 8. Environmental and feedback barriers

John Sterman's [1994 paper *Learning in and about complex systems*](https://doi.org/10.1002/sdr.4260100214) examines how delays, dynamic complexity, ambiguous feedback, flawed mental models, group processes, and defensive routines obstruct learning about real systems.

Feedback can be incomplete, lagged, selectively observed, or produced partly by the measuring system itself. An institution may repeatedly adjust toward a misleading metric while worsening what the metric leaves out.

Important caution: this need not arise from bad intentions. It can arise from measurement design, resource constraints, technical complexity, incentives, or legitimate competing values.

So **learning ability** and **feedback quality** have to be examined separately.

## 10. Falsification and maintenance limits

This inquiry should be narrowed if:
- the proposed "governing assumption" cannot be specified or observed in practice;
- only documents change, not observable decisions or behavior;
- a competent existing organizational-learning assessment finds the same relevant mechanisms with less overhead;
- changing the governing variable adds no explanatory power over ordinary resource/accessibility constraints;
- revising the goal worsens outcomes without a justified decision basis or creates unacceptable procedural burdens;
- the social context makes the proposed observation, disclosure or participation inappropriate;
- observers create contamination such that the change cannot be distinguished from measurement effects.

**Do not interpret failure to change as always pathological.** Stable rules, legitimate refusal, and durable safeguards can be essential. A system should be able to reconsider a goal without being obliged to change it.

## 11. What we should retain

> A system can learn to optimize its actions without learning to question its governing assumptions.

> An institution's stated values are not the same as the values expressed by its actual choices.

> The capacity to revise goals is not the same as the right to impose new goals on others.

> Greater intelligence may help explain a system's constraints, but it cannot eliminate the question of who may change them.

These are working formulations. Their explanatory value must be compared against the established methods and evidence cited above.
