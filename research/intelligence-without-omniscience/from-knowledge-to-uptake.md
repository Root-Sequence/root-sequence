> A system can contain knowledge relevant to correcting itself while lacking the means, incentives, categories, or legitimate authority to act on that knowledge.

This is a **research hypothesis and diagnostic starting point**, not a law. Sometimes relevant knowledge truly is absent, an observation is mistaken, or the requested action is unjustified. A responsible method distinguishes those cases.

## 1. A crucial correction to "nobody understands the whole problem"

A complex problem need not be entirely understood by one participant for meaningful progress to occur. Partial knowledge is normal. But there are several distinct failure modes:

| Failure | Example | Relevant established work | Question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Missing evidence | Nobody knows whether the route's platform is usable | Measurement, inquiry design | How can evidence be obtained ethically and affordably? |
| Knowledge discoverability | A relevant manual or discipline cannot be found | Information retrieval, information foraging | Can unfamiliar questions locate expert vocabulary and sources? |
| Expertise routing | A group cannot identify which member knows the constraint | Transactive memory | Does it know who knows what, and are these beliefs accurate? |
| Information integration | A group repeats common facts and never shares crucial unique ones | Hidden-profile studies | Which procedure brings relevant differences into view? |
| Credibility injustice | A reliable witness is discounted due to prejudice | Fricker and social epistemology | Why was testimony discounted, and with what evidence of unfairness? |
| Interpretive exclusion | Official forms cannot express a relevant experience | Hermeneutical injustice and classification studies | Who had power to define the categories? |
| Uptake failure | A sound criticism is acknowledged but has no opportunity to revise the model | Helen Longino's critical contextual empiricism | Can criticism make a difference to inquiry? |
| Conversion failure | Participants understand the remedy but lack money, tools, accessible routes, or authorization | Capability approach | What makes the resource into an achievable opportunity? |
| Legitimate disagreement | Parties know the facts but disagree about goals or authority | Political philosophy, deliberation | Who may decide, with what process and constraints? |
| Epistemic exploitation | People are repeatedly expected to educate an institution at personal cost | Nora Berenstain and related critique | Who pays for the system to learn? |

No single label should stand in for diagnosis. Multiple failure modes may be present; the correct remedy differs by mechanism.

## 2. Transactive memory is more than a directory

[Argote & Ren 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-6486.2012.01077.x) survey transactive memory systems as a knowledge-sharing capability in organizations. An operational characterization includes **specialization**, **credibility regarding task expertise**, and **coordination**.

"Who knows what?" is a useful shorthand but incomplete:
- The group's beliefs about expertise can be false or outdated.
- An individual can possess relevant expertise but be unavailable or unwilling to contribute.
- An actor can know a technical claim without knowing whether that claim is ethically or politically decisive.
- A shared directory may improve access while becoming a surveillance or coercion mechanism.
- The credibility of an expert within one domain must not become universal authority over other domains.

Working design question: could a knowledge-routing system provide a **contestable map of expertise and sources** without demanding a centralized registry of people and identities?

### Possible tests

Two teams share the same specialists, time budgets, and knowledge, but differ in whether they have a credible map of expertise; induce one deliberately stale mapping; track information sought, misrouting, correction, and downstream actions. This would test particular routing mechanisms, not prove a general intelligence factor.

## 3. Hidden profiles and the importance of unshared information

[Stasser & Titus 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.48.6.1467) investigated how groups discuss shared versus unique information. A later [meta-analysis by Lu, Yuan & McLeod 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21896790/) examines 25 years of hidden-profile experiments.

The lesson is not "all groups are irrational." It is that information collectively **available** to group members is not the same as information **surfaced** or **integrated** into deliberation.

Tests must distinguish:
- information that was never held by anyone;
- information held but unshared;
- information shared but dismissed for defensible reasons;
- information incorrectly translated;
- information reasonably withheld due to consent, risk, or scope;
- information accepted but lacking actionable means.

More speaking time or larger attendance is not proof that the group used its unique information well.

## 4. Uptake: the difference between a suggestion box and corrigible inquiry

[Helen Longino's critical contextual empiricism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-knowledge-social/) emphasizes four conditions for fruitful communal criticism:
1. recognized avenues to criticize evidence, assumptions, methods, and conclusions;
2. **uptake of criticism** through demonstrable responsiveness of an epistemic community;
3. shared, publicly accessible standards for criticism and assessment;
4. **tempered equality of intellectual authority**, without pretending all claims have identical expertise or evidentiary strength.

These norms concern **epistemic communities**, especially science. Translating them to government, AI product feedback, or local governance requires care because scientific objectivity and legitimate political authorization are different problems.

The proposed diagnostic is not simply "Did officials collect feedback?" but:
- Was criticism recognized in its actual form?
- Was it evaluated against clear standards?
- If sound and relevant, could it revise the assumptions, model, options, or decision?
- If rejected, were the grounds understandable and open to challenge?
- Were unresolved evidence gaps recorded?
- Was the result revisited after consequences emerged?

Uptake does **not** mean uncritically accepting any submitted claim. The possibility of warranted rejection is a necessary part of reliable inquiry.

The subtle point: a system may optimize its collection of human feedback while being incapable of changing the assumptions that govern its response. It can become better at *hearing* without becoming better at *learning*.

## 5. Boundary objects: coordination without one interpretation

[Star & Griesemer 1989](https://doi.org/10.1177/030631289019003001) studied scientific collaboration across amateur collectors, professional researchers, administrators, and others associated with UC Berkeley's Museum of Vertebrate Zoology. They developed the notion of **boundary objects**: stable enough to coordinate across social worlds but adaptable to distinct local uses and meanings. Examples include standardized forms, repositories, and maps or other representations whose identity is maintained across perspectives.

A shared neighborhood map, a repair log, a source-backed event timeline, or a scenario data packet **might** work as a boundary object.

It need not erase different interpretations:
- transit engineers see capacities and routes;
- riders see lived time and reliability;
- disabled residents see continuous access or barriers;
- maintainers see dependencies and work;
- local organisms are represented through ecological evidence rather than being presumed to share human goals.

A map that only represents vehicle throughput could also suppress essential knowledge. Thus the question is not "Can everyone view the map?" but also **"Who can dispute its categories and revise its representation?"**

Important limit: not every shared spreadsheet, map, or object is a boundary object in the full sociological sense. Treat that classification as an empirical question about social uses and coordinated work, not a decorative label.

### A notable historical bridge

The 1989 origin in a natural-history museum is particularly relevant to Root Sequence's interests in knowledge preservation and [the Museum of Ordinary Life](https://museumofordinarylife.org/), but resemblance is not permission to reuse archival materials or impose a governance role. The original paper studies how heterogeneous contributors cooperated, not a general license to pool historical knowledge into AI datasets.

[Susan Leigh Star 1999](https://doi.org/10.1177/00027649921955326) also emphasizes infrastructure as relational, mundane, and embedded in work practices. Its effectiveness may appear invisible until breakdowns expose who depended on it.

## 6. Epistemic injustice isn't a synonym for disagreement

[Miranda Fricker 2007](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780198237907.001.0001) distinguishes prejudicial credibility deficits (**testimonial injustice**) and structural gaps in shared interpretive resources that unfairly disadvantage some people's understanding/communication (**hermeneutical injustice**).

[Gaile Pohlhaus Jr. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1527-2001.2011.01222.x) sharpens the latter inquiry through **willful hermeneutical ignorance**: dominant knowers refusing to acknowledge interpretive resources developed by marginalized knowers.

This matters because an institution's claim that "we lack a vocabulary for this" may be inaccurate. People most affected may already have names and ways of describing the phenomenon that dominant institutions decline to engage.

[Kristie Dotson 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1527-2001.2011.01177.x) examines testimonial silencing and smothering—why speech may be constrained by the audience's inability or unwillingness to receive it appropriately.

Crucial distinction: **fair evidence weighting ≠ prejudicial discounting**. A speaker may be mistaken; a professional may know facts a resident cannot directly observe; direct experience may reveal effects no official model represents. Specific claims require evidence appropriate to each.

The design target is **correctable representation and fair evaluation**, not uniform credibility or forced consensus.

## 7. Epistemic exploitation: who's doing the unpaid work of correction?

[Nora Berenstain 2016](https://doi.org/10.3998/ergo.12405314.0003.022) develops **epistemic exploitation**, examining coercive demands that marginalized people perform uncompensated, taxing educational labor for privileged people.

This is not a general prohibition on asking questions. It identifies a specific pattern of inequitable labor and social power. It is highly relevant to participatory system design: inviting people to "improve our model" can become extractive if the same contributors are continually required to re-explain their existence, justify credibility, or correct institutions that impose costs on them.

Research questions:
- Who was expected to identify and explain the omission?
- Could an institution have done its own preparatory research?
- Were participants paid, supported, or given actual decision rights?
- Could they decline to educate without losing access, standing, or services?
- Who maintained and corrected the knowledge system afterward?
- Did the process reduce future repetitive demands—or institutionalize them?

**Design constraint:** the burden of making an institution able to understand people must not automatically fall on the people its present categories exclude.

## 8. Resource availability versus substantive capability

[The capability approach](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/) associated with Amartya Sen and developed by others distinguishes resources, **conversion factors**, achieved functionings, and substantive opportunities.

A training manual is a resource. Effective opportunity to repair equipment also depends on accessible instruction, tools, time, physical conditions, and permission to do the repair.

In Root Sequence terms, knowledge might pass through:
**available → findable → intelligible → evaluable → actionable → contestable**.

This is a *proposed diagnostic route*, not Sen's official taxonomy, and it need not be a linear ladder. Real-world cases loop and have multiple constraints simultaneously.

The normative choice of which capabilities matter belongs to the appropriate people and institutions; it cannot be supplied by cognitive metrics alone.

## 9. Who defines the problem?

Often the greatest influence occurs *before* group problem-solving begins: when one institution defines the question, admissible evidence, possible interventions, or target performance indicator.

[Landemore 2012](https://doi.org/10.23943/princeton/9780691155654.003.0004) argues for the contribution of cognitive diversity to deliberation. Its application is disputed. [Jonathan Benson 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-021-03161-0) challenges overly strong claims using complexity and the assumptions of the diversity-trumps-ability model. A [2024 American Political Science Review analysis](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/democracy-and-the-epistemic-problems-of-political-polarization/A1B13D1D4845FE5A440CCD3F5895063F) further emphasizes the political role of **problem discovery**, not just deciding among predefined options.

Concrete difference:
- "How do we increase transit throughput?" prioritizes system efficiency.
- "Who cannot reach essential destinations?" reveals time and accessibility constraints.
- "Whose labor and space would the proposed change displace?" surfaces distribution.
- "Who can change the conditions producing these difficulties?" investigates authority and structural alternatives.

These may all be worthwhile, but no single one includes all the others. A system can solve its chosen optimization target while neglecting a different problem experienced by affected people.

**Research intervention:** invite participants to nominate and contest the problem formulation *before* presenting a bounded options menu. Compare that with a procedure that offers only predefined solutions. The outcomes can include newly recognized problems, cost, delay, disagreement, or simply no material change.

## 10. The infrastructure recursion

A feedback relationship worth investigating:

1. Cognitive capacities and available knowledge influence what actors can construct or question.
2. Shared records, standards, interfaces, methods, and organizations change the conditions for later inquiry.
3. These conditions alter who can learn, contribute, and act, and whose labor is necessary.
4. Resulting practices can reinforce or change the infrastructure, selection pressures, incentives, and decision rights.

This is not automatically a beneficial reinforcing loop. Greater capacity can also centralize knowledge, obscure dissent, create dependency, intensify surveillance, or impose maintenance obligations.

[Meadows' leverage-points tradition](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11625-022-01188-x) distinguishes changing information flows, rules, self-organization, and goals as different interventions into a system. Use this as an analytical guide rather than a guarantee about the effectiveness of any proposed intervention.

[Root Sequence Intelligence Ecology](../../concepts/intelligence-ecology.md) already asks what behaviors an environment rewards; this inquiry adds the *different* question of what conditions make particular cognitive capabilities usable. What is incentivized, what is accessible, what is legitimate, and what is good must remain separate axes.

## 10A. When external knowledge feels like personal understanding

An important counterweight to "intelligence as infrastructure" is the **illusion of explanatory depth**. [Rozenblit & Keil (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2605_1) demonstrated that people can overestimate the depth of their own mechanistic understanding until asked to explain a process in detail. [Fisher, Goddu & Keil (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000070) experimentally found that Internet searches for explanatory knowledge can increase estimates of *internal* knowledge—blurring what a person knows and what they can retrieve externally.

These studies do not establish that AI always impairs thinking, that participants are personally deficient, or that every task warrants memorization. Rather they highlight a **calibration problem**.

Distinguish at least four states:
- *I understand the mechanism sufficiently to explain and interrogate it.*
- *I know where the appropriate account or expert is, and can access it reliably.*
- *I can follow the explanation with help but cannot independently reconstruct it.*
- *I am merely confident because fluent explanations or external tools are nearby.*

All four can have practical value in appropriate circumstances, but they are not interchangeable. Offloading unnecessary memorization can be sensible; falsely claiming understanding is hazardous when taking consequential actions.

For human–AI collaboration, a possible lightweight method is to ask:
1. What do I actually understand?
2. What am I relying on a source, expert, or model to supply?
3. Which assumptions or evidence would I need to check before acting?
4. If the tool disappeared or disagreed with another source, what would remain usable?
5. Is a handoff to an actual specialist more appropriate than further summarization?

These prompts should be *optional and proportionate*, not an examination imposed on all users. Independence is not a moral requirement and accommodations are not intellectual failures.

**Proposed test:** Compare source retrieval, AI summary, and source-backed explanation for a bounded task. Separately measure task performance, confidence calibration, ability to identify unsupported claims, willingness to seek expertise, retained explanation when relevant, and the ability to choose not to explain. Do not mistake a better score on a memory recall test for a complete evaluation of cognitive augmentation.

[Mechanisms of collective knowledge](mechanisms-of-collective-knowledge.md) supplies a separate source-aware causal investigation. Its analytic fact-coverage probe explicitly stops at whether relevant facts enter the shared record; it does **not** represent uptake, justification, power, or consequences. This document retains those distinct questions.

## 11. Paired Coherent World tests with invalidation conditions

**Test A — information routing:** identical actors, task, information, and group size; vary whether the group has an accurate, stale, or unavailable expertise map. Measure whether critical knowledge is actually obtained, and at what cost.

**Test B — criticism without/with uptake:** provide the same objection through different institutional procedures. Compare whether the objection can revise assumptions, questions, and feasible options rather than merely populate a feedback log. Include a deliberately weak objection to confirm that "uptake" is not identical to uncritical acceptance.

**Test C — boundary-object legibility:** provide different actors one shared map. Compare a static official-only category model with a contestable multi-perspective representation. Track what remains invisible, who can edit, what new work is imposed, and whether the same underlying event maintains traceable identity.

**Test D — problem framing:** same neighborhood facts and resources; compare an agency-predefined optimization question with a process allowing affected actors to articulate alternate problems. Track recognized needs, output validity, costs, distribution, and whether the new framing changes available interventions.

**Test E — epistemic labor and refusal:** a participant has information relevant to the decision but reasonably refuses compulsory disclosure. Compare lawful and consent-respecting alternatives (independent accessible data, paid consultation, alternative design) without inventing private facts.

**Test F — AI mediator:** compare original documents and testimony with a summarizer that may (through authored failure modes) omit a minority concern, invent a consensus, or flatten a term. Require source traceability, original-speaker review when authorized, and a non-AI option. Separate source-recognition outcomes from decision legitimacy.

**Test G — degradation:** lose the AI assistant, the maintainer, or the central directory. Examine whether inquiry and repair remain possible and what infrastructure proved indispensable.

*Null and disconfirming results:* a simple informed expert may outperform deliberation; contested diagrams may slow decisions without improving accuracy; a data-rich group may still lack resources or authority; high-quality AI may improve technical accuracy but compromise privacy; additional meetings may unjustifiably burden people. Report these as genuine outcomes. A simulated outcome reflects authored rules until independently validated.

## 12. Three layers of accountability

A plausible research/output architecture should preserve:

**Source layer:** who contributed which original observation, document, measurement, or proposed interpretation—and under which permissions and epistemic status.

**Translation layer:** how the contribution became a summary, category, map, model input, or decision option; which distinctions were preserved or lost; how to challenge that translation.

**Decision layer:** who was legitimately authorized to act, the actual evidence/arguments used, alternatives excluded, affected-party objections, consequences, and review or reversal conditions.

Do not use detailed provenance as justification for indiscriminate surveillance. A source can be anonymized, selectively disclosed, or excluded from retention while still being evaluated through an appropriate process. Full public visibility is not required for every trustworthy process.

## 13. How to test whether this synthesis adds value

Against competent native baselines (accessibility audit, informed deliberation, organizational knowledge map, fair evidence review, ordinary incident analysis):
- Does the multi-domain synthesis expose a consequential missing mechanism that baseline work overlooks?
- Does it improve the *choice of what to investigate*, not merely add more questions?
- Can an uninformed reader understand what specific claim the synthesis makes and what would falsify it?
- Does it distinguish factual from value and authorization disagreements?
- Does it cost substantially more analyst time than it saves?
- Can the method be *narrowed or retired* if it does not improve a specific research task?

If established tools already capture the necessary mechanisms, their work should be credited; the contribution may be **routing between tools**, not a newly named phenomenon.

## 15. Durable working formulations

> Knowledge being present is not the same as knowledge being recognized.  
> Recognition is not the same as epistemic uptake.  
> Uptake is not the same as authorization.  
> Authorization is not the same as justified action.  
> And technically capable action is not necessarily a good outcome.

> A group need not share one mind to share a problem. But it may need shared, corrigible ways of finding relevant knowledge and revising what it thought the problem was.

> Do not make the excluded pay endlessly for the institution's ability to understand them.

These formulations are exploratory synthesis, not proof of novelty or direct quotes from scholarly sources.
