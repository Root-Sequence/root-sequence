# Intelligence commons
## A governance and infrastructure inquiry, not a universal data pool

**Document role:** Focused research/design question within [Intelligence Without Omniscience](README.md).  
**Status:** OPEN PROPOSAL / AI-assisted / review-pending, 2026-10-07.  
**Not:** an incorporated institution, a product promise, authorization to scrape or pool data, a blanket open-source requirement, a proposed central AI authority, or a governance charter for any existing Root Sequence community project.

## The question

> What would it take for knowledge, tools, learning, and coordination to become widely usable capabilities—not merely resources held by those able to pay, search with expert vocabulary, or obtain institutional permission?

And the equally important counter-question:

> What if the infrastructure making knowledge accessible also makes people easier to surveil, exploit, exclude, or control?

Neither question can be answered by openness alone.

## What the term might mean

An **intelligence commons** is a provisional shorthand for the *shared, governed conditions supporting people's ability to discover, evaluate, produce, retain, exchange, and use knowledge*. It is not a declaration that all knowledge is one common asset or that groups constitute one superintelligence.

It builds on rather than replaces:
- knowledge commons and research on commons governance (Charlotte Hess / Elinor Ostrom);
- libraries, archives, education, documentation, free software, accessible interfaces, public science, and research institutions;
- social epistemology, participatory knowledge production, and civic infrastructures;
- Root Sequence's Epistemic Discoverability, Legible Systems, Intelligence Ecology, and Design Grammar.

A good commons may be a **federation of many differently governed resources**, some public, some consent-restricted, some local, some offline, some temporary, some explicitly private. The existence of shared standards need not give any one operator power over all participants.

## A resource does not guarantee a capability

Distinguish:
1. **Exists:** research, a manual, local know-how, or a tested method has been recorded.
2. **Discoverable:** the right person can find it from ordinary language or an unfamiliar question.
3. **Accessible:** it can be encountered through suitable formats, languages, devices, and bodily circumstances.
4. **Interpretable:** relevant claims can be understood without hiding limitations or reassigning authority.
5. **Evaluable:** sources, methods, disagreements, and uncertainty are recoverable.
6. **Applicable:** time, materials, permissions, collaboration, or other practical supports exist.
7. **Revisable:** a user can contest conclusions, correct records, replace services, and repair errors.
8. **Governable:** the relevant communities and rights holders can shape the conditions of use and reuse.

These are interacting dimensions rather than a rigid checklist or new scalar. Public access to a PDF may fail nearly all of them.

## Research anchors and boundaries

**Hess and Ostrom:** [Understanding Knowledge as a Commons](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262516037/understanding-knowledge-as-a-commons/) (2007) studies opportunities for, and threats to, shared knowledge resources. Their work is relevant to institutional design, not an endorsement of this new phrase.

**UNESCO:** Its [2021 Recommendation on Open Science](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-open-science) includes open infrastructures, engagement with societal actors, knowledge sharing, and participation. Its collective-intelligence language is particularly relevant. But it is a recommendation principally to member states on *open science*, not a blanket legal order to private AI labs and not a rule requiring publication of everything.

**Open-source AI:** The [Open Source Initiative definition](https://opensource.org/ai) distinguishes freedoms to use, study, modify, and share an AI system. "Open weights," free product access, API access, and genuinely open-source AI are different technical/legal conditions.

**Privacy and restricted knowledge:** The [Global Indigenous Data Alliance's CARE Principles](https://www.gida-global.org/careprinciples) stress Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, and Ethics. They were developed to complement data-centric open/FAIR principles by putting Indigenous rights, interests, and governance at the center. This is a direct warning against equating the commons with compulsory availability of Indigenous knowledge or others' sensitive data. Root Sequence should learn from and cite Indigenous-led governance rather than appropriating authority to speak for it.

**Human–AI performance:** [Vaccaro et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1) show that working with AI does not automatically outperform the best standalone participant. Any AI-mediated commons design needs task-level evaluation, not simply a claim of "collective intelligence."

## A spectrum of governance, not open versus closed

| Resource | Possible access model | Core governance question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Public science article | Open publication with attribution and correction | Who can understand and check it? |
| Open technical documentation | Free access, forkable or versioned where appropriate | Can people repair the system without one vendor? |
| Community oral history | Scoped consent, context and reuse limits | Who decides audience, duration, and future uses? |
| Indigenous-held knowledge | Rights-holder-defined governance | Whose authority is recognized; who benefits? |
| Local mutual-aid directory | Selective disclosure, expiring data | Can coordination happen without exposing vulnerable members? |
| AI research assistant | Inspectable sources, ability to question/replace | Does the interface enhance user judgment or become a permanent gatekeeper? |
| High-risk research material | Controlled researcher access and review when warranted | How are safety claims independently challengeable without reckless release? |

Access is not always binary. One can permit reading while prohibiting redistribution, permit research without model training, or allow a contributor to withdraw future uses. Actual enforceability, jurisdiction, and technical deletion constraints require separate analysis; software permissions cannot alone guarantee all these policies.

## Infrastructure that would matter

A candidate *non-prescriptive* architecture:

- **Question interface:** start with what a person can express, not a mandatory expert term.
- **Conceptual routing:** show relevant domains and why each may fit or not fit.
- **Source identity and provenance:** distinguish source evidence from AI synthesis; support conflicting claims.
- **Human-understandable representation:** layers from short explanation to raw methods and uncertainty.
- **Transactive knowledge directories:** help find people, documentation, archives, and tools without turning those people into mandatory on-call resources.
- **Correction and appeal:** original authors and affected people can dispute interpretation and misuse.
- **Permissioned reuse:** contextual audiences, separate consent for training, meaningful expiration/withdrawal where achievable.
- **Offline and alternative paths:** no compulsory AI account or single model gateway.
- **Maintenance and steward support:** include labor, funding, operations, governance, and graceful failure.
- **Distributed control:** clarify who can change indexes, rankings, access rules, source versions, or community boundaries.
- **Learning and creation:** reduce entry barriers not just for consumption of published knowledge but for generating and testing contributions.

These are hypotheses for evaluation, not a requirement that all communities adopt the same interface or protocol.

## Intelligence, expertise, and legitimate authority

An institution's throughput, computing power, and prediction accuracy are not equivalent to its legitimacy. Even an AI that makes unusually accurate forecasts does not thereby acquire the right to define collective goals, decide who may be heard, or authorize coercion.

No structure can substitute entirely for political and ethical choices:
- Whose problem is being solved?
- Who defined the task and chose the success measure?
- Who bears the risk?
- Who is represented, who is absent, and who chooses not to participate?
- Which forms of expertise are relevant to which claims?
- Who can challenge the framing and propose alternatives?
- How are maintenance and benefits distributed?
- Who can change the governance arrangement later?

A good knowledge infrastructure can assist deliberation. It cannot make legitimacy disappear.

## Explicit anti-patterns

1. **The total registry:** a universal searchable graph that requires everyone's identity, knowledge, and relationships.
2. **The benevolent monopolist:** one AI mediates every question and all routes for contest or appeal.
3. **Open-washing:** proprietary control behind free access, restricted "open" licenses, or unverifiable claims.
4. **Extractive participation:** people asked to supply expertise and context without rights, credit, compensation, or relevant control.
5. **Consent collapse:** public posting is treated as permission for every future use.
6. **Indexing domination:** one vocabulary or ranking system is mistaken for the shape of reality.
7. **Synthesis without provenance:** machine-generated interpretations displace source voices.
8. **Access without conversion:** research is theoretically available but practically unusable.
9. **Invisible labor:** accessible interfaces appear free because maintainers or interpreters absorb uncompensated burdens.
10. **Performance absolutism:** high accuracy is taken as evidence that the system is fair, accountable, or authorized.
11. **Interoperability theater:** export formats exist but cannot be used meaningfully without the original provider.
12. **Permanent disclosure:** private data is retained indefinitely because future research might benefit.

## Comparative test proposals

**Test 1 — Discoverability with bounded privacy.** Can a novice discover relevant public sources without revealing unnecessary personal details or being forced into the preferred AI product?

**Test 2 — Knowledge routing and source challenge.** Can a question evolve through unexpected fields while preserving original authors, uncertainty, counterevidence, and the ability to leave the recommender?

**Test 3 — Community authority.** Can a group share some knowledge across a federation while retaining its own consent model, refusing AI training reuse, and correcting an incorrect summary?

**Test 4 — Provider outage or exit.** What knowledge, contacts, citations, decisions, and practices survive loss of the service?

**Test 5 — Expert and resident disagreement.** Does the institution distinguish technical modeling competence, direct lived observation, value disagreement, and legitimate decision power without flattening either side?

**Test 6 — Cost accounting.** Does the proposed commons actually reduce the total work required to access and apply knowledge, or move the work onto less visible contributors?

A true trial requires defined participants and tasks, ethical/legal review where relevant, consent, comparison groups, appropriate baselines, and budgets. Coherent World's speculative paper experiments can clarify mechanisms but cannot substitute for empirical verification.

## The enduring question

> What conditions make knowledge and intelligence-related capability meaningfully available—without requiring omniscient individuals, compulsory transparency, exclusive gatekeepers, or concentrated authority?

The provisional word **commons** is only useful if concrete designs can make that question more testable than existing methods already do. If not, retain the questions and retire the terminology.
