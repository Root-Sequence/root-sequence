# Reading Trails

**Document role:** Curated research-rabbit-hole index  
**Status:** Developing; new starter trails are AI-assisted and author review pending  
**Purpose:** Preserve the sites, archives, societies, reading lists, courses, magazines, papers, and other intellectual neighborhoods that surface during Root Sequence research.

## Why this exists

A bibliography answers:

> What source supports this claim?

A Reading Trail answers a different question:

> **Where can someone go wander, learn the field's language, discover adjacent ideas, and find things we did not already know to search for?**

Root Sequence research repeatedly encounters valuable resources that are not themselves the canonical evidence for a claim:

- old specialist magazines;
- scholar-maintained archives;
- societies and conference proceedings;
- reading lists;
- course platforms;
- historical collections;
- research-community wikis;
- practitioner notes;
- repositories of talks and workshops.

Those are easy to lose when only the final paper citation survives.

Reading Trails preserve them.

## How to wander

**Just curious?** Pick a question below, open one entry point, and follow an unfamiliar term. Reading the whole trail is not a prerequisite for using it.

**Investigating a claim?** Use the [Method & Concept Router](../method-router.md) to identify the relevant discipline, then follow a trail into an actual paper, dataset, archive item, or documented case. Record evidence in the bounded inquiry or canonical treatment, not only here.

**Making something?** Follow a trail into a small observation, comparison, or prototype. Bring back what changed the design, including findings that complicate the original idea.

These are suggested paths, not a curriculum, ranking of fields, or obligation to turn every curiosity into a project.

## Rules

A Reading Trail should distinguish:

- **primary / scholarly source** — use for claims when appropriate; inspect the particular work and its publication status;
- **reference / overview** — useful for orientation and terminology;
- **archive** — valuable historical/source material; collection descriptions are not the same as individual records;
- **community / society** — people, events, current work, bibliographies;
- **learning hub** — courses/tutorials;
- **practitioner resource** — useful synthesis whose authority must still be checked;
- **rabbit-hole site** — high discovery value even when it is not itself evidence;
- **data / technical reference** — datasets, schemas, standards, or documentation; not a guarantee of completeness or fitness for a task;
- **advocacy / situated perspective** — a declared position, organizing tradition, or proposed programme; useful without being a neutral account;
- **experimental / speculative work** — prototypes and possible futures; not evidence that a design works generally.

A resource can have more than one role. These labels describe **how to use it**, not a single ladder of prestige.

Do not treat inclusion as endorsement or validation.

Do not replace claim-level evidence with a cool website.

Preserve disagreements and domain boundaries. Similar vocabulary does not establish a shared mechanism. A society's homepage does not establish consensus; a compelling case does not establish general effectiveness.

## Routes across trails

**Build a more humane computing environment:** HCI and accessibility → repair and hardware autonomy → information discovery → the commons. Ask separately about usability, maintainability, knowledge, and governance.

**Explore a community of research and creation:** alternative institutions → commons and mutual aid → HCI's collaborative environments → archives of daily practice. Look at membership, money, maintenance, disagreement, and exit—not only the inspiring output.

**Investigate continuity and change:** consciousness → systems and adaptation → archives → a bounded, domain-specific inquiry. Do not infer that continuity of a person, organization, and dataset means the same thing.

**Build an atlas or simulation:** urban systems → source-data limitations → commons governance → the [future simulation route](future-trails.md#simulation-games-and-causal-models). A convincing map or model is not yet a validated account of lived conditions.

## Future use

When research uncovers a genuinely useful intellectual neighborhood, add it here rather than relying on chat history.

The [Future Trails map](future-trails.md) preserves twelve further directions, each with a question, search vocabulary, a bounded first expedition, and a caution. They are candidates, not completed trails or twelve new project commitments.

Deepen an existing trail before splitting it. A new page is useful when it offers a genuinely different route, not merely another name for the same collection.

## Adding an entry

Keep entries small enough to read and useful enough to act on. Include the resource name and link, its source role, why it belongs, somewhere specific to start, and vocabulary or references to follow next. Note important access limits or reasons not to overread it.

For a new trail, add an ordinary-language question, a handful of complementary resources, a suggested route, a disagreement or failure mode, and links to neighboring trails. A small primary-source doorway, a learning resource, and a practitioner or archival perspective often make a better start than a long list of prestigious institutions.

Record the date and **depth** of checking. Opening a landing page is not reading every paper it links to. Search-index confirmation is not full-page inspection. A blocked retrieval does not prove a resource has disappeared.

Bring substantive findings back through the [research method](../method.md). Keep project-specific implementation in its canonical home and use the [ecosystem map](../../ECOSYSTEM.md) for routing. Public branches and pull requests are public; do not copy private notes or unpublished canon into a trail.

Keep these **curated**, not exhaustive link dumps.
