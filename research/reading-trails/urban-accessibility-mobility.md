# Urban Systems, Accessibility & Mobility

**Document role:** Reading Trail  
**Status:** Starter; AI-assisted curation; author review pending  
**Entry-point check:** 2026-09-25; discovery-level, not a field survey

> **What makes a place or journey usable, rather than merely present on a map?**

This trail connects mapping, public-space practice, transport data, and access. Keep the distinction between an object, its recorded attributes, the route to it, and someone's ability to use it.

## 1. OpenStreetMap Wiki — Map Features

[Feature vocabulary and tagging routes](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/Map_features)  
**Type:** community-maintained technical reference

A starting point for understanding how physical features are represented in a shared map. Follow a feature into its specific tag documentation and examples before designing a parallel vocabulary.

**Start with:** benches, drinking water, toilets, paths, crossings, and other everyday infrastructure. Ask which attributes matter beyond location.

**Follow into:** tagging, access, opening hours, survey provenance, feature relationships, data quality.

**Limit:** a documented tag is not evidence that the relevant places have been surveyed. Check current contribution, import, licensing, and attribution guidance separately before reusing or submitting data.

## 2. Taskar Center — OpenSidewalks, TDEI & AccessMap

[Project documentation](https://taskarcenteratuw.github.io/tcat-wiki/)  
**Type:** research/practitioner hub / data and routing documentation

A useful doorway into modeling pedestrian networks and access requirements. The documentation distinguishes the OpenSidewalks schema, datasets, the data-exchange platform, and routing tools rather than treating them as one product.

**Follow into:** connected sidewalk graphs, crossings, curb ramps, pedestrian routing, data validation, personalized access requirements.

**Limit:** a schema or tool's existence does not imply coverage for a particular city, current conditions, or suitability for every pedestrian. Check the specific dataset and its scope.

## 3. General Transit Feed Specification

[Schedule reference](https://gtfs.org/documentation/schedule/reference/)  
**Type:** technical specification / data reference

A route into how transit services are represented as structured data. Use the reference to understand the records behind a map or journey planner, including how a particular field relates to a question you want to answer.

**Follow into:** stops, routes, service calendars, trips, transfers, pathways, accessibility fields.

**Limit:** scheduled service is not observed service. A supplied field is not a guarantee of its accuracy, completeness, or the physical accessibility of an end-to-end journey.

## 4. NACTO — Urban Street Design Guide

[Guide](https://nacto.org/publication/urban-street-design-guide/)  
**Type:** practitioner design reference

A doorway into the choices embedded in street layouts. Browse a street element or design problem and compare its intended function with what a person would encounter on the ground.

**Follow into:** crossings, sidewalks, street allocation, traffic calming, intersections, public space.

**Limit:** a design guide does not establish local conditions or replace the applicable local standards and engineering review.

## 5. Project for Public Spaces

[Public-space practice and resources](https://www.pps.org/)  
**Type:** practitioner hub / placemaking perspective

Useful for asking what people actually do in shared places and how spaces support or exclude different uses. Follow a place or practice into observation questions rather than evaluating it only through attractive photographs.

**Follow into:** public life, sociability, seating, everyday use, participation, stewardship.

**Limit:** an appealing public-space story is not a substitute for evidence about access, displacement, maintenance, or who feels welcome.

## Suggested expeditions

**Make an amenities atlas:** OSM vocabulary → a small field survey → compare recorded attributes with observed use conditions → preserve unknowns and observation dates.

**Study a whole journey:** GTFS service information → pedestrian-network documentation → an end-to-end access question → appropriate field verification.

**Investigate a public place:** NACTO or PPS → one design hypothesis → observations at different times → feedback from people with different access needs.

## Bring back a bounded finding

For one public amenity, distinguish: mapped, recently observed, reachable, available at the relevant time, and usable for a specified need. Treat those as separate fields or questions rather than a single universal accessibility score.

Do not infer absence from missing map data. Do not publish precise locations of vulnerable people or sensitive community resources merely because a mapping workflow can capture them. Gather only the data needed for the public-purpose question.
