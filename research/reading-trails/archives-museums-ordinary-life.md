# Archives, Museums & Ordinary Life

**Document role:** Reading Trail  
**Status:** Starter; AI-assisted curation; author review pending  
**Entry-point check:** 2026-09-25; discovery-level, not a collections audit

> **How do we preserve ordinary life while retaining the context, permissions, and relationships that make a record meaningful?**

Begin with the everyday object, account, interface, routine, or sound—not only the exceptional event. Then ask what recording it changes and who should control its later uses.

## 1. Mass Observation

[Archive and project](https://massobs.org.uk/)  
**Type:** archive / everyday-life documentation

A direct entry into accounts of ordinary lives and ways of eliciting them. Explore the archive and project descriptions to ask what people are invited to record, which voices appear, and how the collection is made.

**Follow into:** diaries, everyday experience, prompts, self-documentation, social history, collecting practices.

**Limit:** an archive of participants' accounts is not automatically a representative sample of a population. Preserve the circumstances of collection and avoid turning one account into everyone's experience.

## 2. Oral History Association

[Principles and Best Practices](https://oralhistory.org/principles-and-best-practices-revised-2018/)  
**Type:** professional ethics / methodological guidance

A practical starting point for the relationship between a narrator, interviewer, archive, and future audience. Use the guidance to think through the entire process, not only the moment a recording is made.

**Follow into:** informed consent, narrator participation, preparation, access, preservation, interpretation.

**Limit:** agreement to participate does not erase ongoing ethical questions about context, vulnerability, and reuse. Check the guidance and permissions relevant to the particular project.

## 3. Local Contexts

[Labels, Notices, and community governance](https://localcontexts.org/)  
**Type:** Indigenous knowledge/data-governance initiative / contextual metadata tools

An important route into provenance, cultural authority, and differentiated permissions. Read how the project distinguishes community-managed Labels from Notices used by institutions and researchers.

**Follow into:** Indigenous data sovereignty, Traditional Knowledge and Biocultural Labels, provenance, protocols, access and circulation.

**Limit:** these tools arise from specific communities' rights and governance. Do not copy them into a generic consent system or claim authority to apply a community's Labels on its behalf. Learn from the distinctions without stripping away their context.

## 4. Digital Preservation Coalition

[Digital Preservation Handbook](https://www.dpconline.org/handbook)  
**Type:** professional reference / preservation learning hub

A route from the desire to keep something into the work required to preserve digital material. Follow a preservation problem into the handbook's vocabulary and planning questions.

**Follow into:** preservation planning, integrity, storage, metadata, format risks, organizational responsibility.

**Limit:** making a backup is not a complete preservation programme. A preservation plan also needs people, resources, and tests of whether material remains usable.

## 5. Library of Congress — Sustainability of Digital Formats

[Format descriptions and evaluation factors](https://www.loc.gov/preservation/digital/formats/)  
**Type:** institutional technical reference

A deep rabbit hole into the properties of the files an archive might depend on. Start with sustainability factors, then inspect a format relevant to text, sound, images, moving images, datasets, or web archives.

**Follow into:** disclosure, transparency, self-documentation, external dependencies, format relationships, accessibility.

**Limit:** choosing a documented format does not preserve the surrounding meaning, rights, provenance, or software behavior by itself.

## Suggested expeditions

**Document an ordinary day:** Mass Observation → a carefully scoped prompt → a consent and access plan → one contextualized record.

**Preserve an interface or digital object:** Library of Congress format guidance → DPC planning questions → a restoration or rendering test → a record of what the capture omitted.

**Design permissions without flattening them:** OHA guidance → Local Contexts' distinctions → the actual contributors' needs and authority → a project-specific, reviewable permissions model.

## Bring back a bounded finding

Choose one ordinary artifact. Record what it is, who supplied it, when and how it was documented, what context is missing, who can authorize reuse, and how the record could be corrected.

Treat public display, research access, machine-learning use, and generative reuse as separate decisions rather than inferring blanket consent from availability. Keep the original record distinguishable from later interpretation or synthetic reconstruction.
