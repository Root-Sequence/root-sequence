# Coherence Without Uniformity
## Contextual coherence: shared protections, locally responsive rules

> Share the protections that make living together possible. Let the means respond to the people, place, and consequences. Keep both the rules and the power to revise them accountable.

**Coherence Without Uniformity** names the principle. **Contextual Coherence** is shorthand for its working design question: can different arrangements sustain understandable, fair, workable relationships under their actual conditions? These are two names for this inquiry, not separate frameworks or a new scientific variable.

A common purpose does not always require an identical procedure. A shared procedure does not guarantee a shared protection. The proposal is to distinguish what people should be able to rely on from the particular mechanisms used to provide it—and to investigate when those mechanisms need to be shared, adapted, or replaced.

## 1. Shared protections are a commitment, not an empirical discovery

The proposed common ground includes bodily safety, dignity, meaningful access, non-discrimination, privacy, practical agency, fair process, and the ability to challenge consequential decisions. Ecological dependencies and harms to people outside the decision boundary also belong in the inquiry. Which protections bind a particular institution must be made explicit through legitimate processes and applicable obligations.

**Universal** describes the intended reach of the protection: everyone affected counts, including minorities, newcomers, people unable to attend, and people with little bargaining power. It does not mean humanity has agreed on an exhaustive list, that every institution has identical duties, or that Root Sequence can declare a worldwide constitution. These are normative commitments whose interpretation, tensions, and enforcement remain contestable.

Equivalent concern may require different support. A procedure that everyone can nominally use may still exclude someone who cannot see its signal, afford its fee, attend its meeting, or recover from its failure. Conversely, granting unlimited discretion in the name of difference can make dependable protection disappear.

A local variation should therefore explain how it preserves the relevant protection in practice. Aggregate convenience cannot compensate automatically for an excluded group losing essential access. A rights complaint needs a route beyond the body whose rule is challenged. Neither a local majority nor a central optimizer gets to settle legitimacy by counting favorable outcomes alone.

The protection floor is not beyond criticism. Its content and interpretation can require revision. But changing it needs a distinct, explicit process with a wider affected boundary and stronger justification than changing an operational setting. A routine exception must not quietly become permission to remove the protection itself.

## 2. Context is a causal question

Local responsiveness should identify which differences could change what works:

| Dimension | Questions to investigate |
| --- | --- |
| Physical and ecological | What do terrain, visibility, climate, resource limits, routes, or ecological connections change? |
| Bodies and access | Whose sensory, mobility, cognitive, communication, or care needs alter the practical task? |
| Social and institutional | Which histories, dependencies, trust conditions, obligations, and enforcement practices matter? |
| Material capacity | Who has the money, staffing, time, maintenance, infrastructure, and fallback capacity? |
| Time | Does the rule fit ordinary use, emergencies, seasonal conditions, or a transition between them? |
| Connected places | Who receives displaced traffic, pollution, work, exclusion, or risk beyond the local boundary? |

This is a research map, not evidence that every item matters in every case. Name the proposed mechanism, seek local knowledge and stronger domain evidence, and state what would make the supposed difference irrelevant.

“Local” is not necessarily a municipality or a culturally homogeneous community. A watershed, commuting corridor, access network, or shared service can cross administrative borders. Residents, visitors, workers, and downstream communities may all have standing in different decisions. Choosing the boundary is itself a power-bearing choice.

## 3. Polycentric governance needs coordination and recourse

Ostrom's work is a substantive predecessor: her 2009 lecture examines multiple decision centers and institutional variation, including local fit, participation in modifying rules, monitoring, conflict resolution, and nested arrangements.[^ostrom] It supplies a research lineage, not proof of this proposal or a guarantee that decentralization improves every outcome.

The proposed arrangement distributes decisions while maintaining relationships between them:

- **Decide near the affected situation where feasible.** Situated knowledge and practical responsibility should influence the decision.
- **Coordinate at the scale of the consequence.** Cross-boundary harms, shared resources, and network reliability may require wider decisions.
- **Keep independent recourse.** Local control needs protection against local domination; wider authority needs protection against remote, unaccountable override.
- **Share capacity as well as responsibility.** A poor locality should not receive a demanding mandate without resources while a wealthy one purchases reliable protection.
- **Record who may do what.** Advice, implementation, enforcement, emergency suspension, and amendment are different powers.

This resembles subsidiarity as a design question—what should be handled at which level—but selects no single constitutional form. Delegation can be useful. Some standards should be broadly binding. Each authority needs a defined remit, reasons, limits, review, and a way to contest its boundary.

Participation must affect options or decisions, not merely collect comments. Accessible formats, asynchronous routes, supported representation, paid participation where appropriate, and recorded minority objections are candidate means. Their adequacy needs local assessment. Nobody should have to become a full-time administrator to retain basic protection, and declining a meeting must not be treated as consent to its outcome.

Local inquiry should still contribute to shared learning. Exchange reasons, failures, contextual conditions, resource requirements, and unresolved objections so another place can assess relevance. A promising result elsewhere is a candidate to examine, not a template to copy or permission to bypass local authority. Cooperation can share expertise and infrastructure while preserving the ability to reject an unsuitable transfer.

## 4. Interoperability makes variation usable

Different local rules can impose confusion, duplicated administration, and costs on people crossing boundaries. The proposal therefore combines room for variation with a deliberately limited shared interface:

- discoverable rules with scope, effective dates, and versions;
- consistent meanings for essential signals and warnings;
- clear transitions between jurisdictions or service arrangements;
- compatible ways to request access, challenge decisions, and recover from error;
- documented dependencies, maintenance responsibilities, and failure behavior.

A shared interface is a design obligation to investigate, not a demand for one database, identity system, provider, or surveillance layer. Interoperability must not require disclosing personal information beyond its purpose. Technical compatibility also does not make two institutions' obligations equivalent.

Uniformity is sometimes the appropriate answer. Stable signal meanings, safety requirements, common infrastructure interfaces, and widely reusable procedures may reduce consequential ambiguity. A competent common standard with well-designed accommodations can outperform an elaborate patchwork. Local variation needs to justify its coordination cost; common standards need to justify the differences they suppress.

## 5. The Wiggle: a diagnostic illustration

A July 29, 2015 report describes cyclists on San Francisco's Wiggle stopping individually during a protest, a queue on Steiner Street, and competing safety and legality concerns.[^wiggle] This is a historical account of a demonstration, not a controlled comparison of everyday traffic policies.

The useful question is broader than whether cyclists should stop: **what arrangements protect people using this intersection while making movement understandable and workable?** The following is a proposed analysis, not a description of conditions verified on today's Wiggle.

Start with protections: a person crossing should not have to negotiate physically with approaching traffic to exercise priority; riders should not have to rely on aggressive movement to remain viable; drivers, delivery workers, and visitors need legible expectations. Examine wheelchair access, blind and low-vision crossing needs, children, older people, different cycling abilities and equipment, emergency access, and those using connecting streets.

Then investigate mechanisms: sight lines, approach speeds, crossing distances, gradients, volumes, turning movements, signal recognition, congestion, and enforcement. Do not assume every cyclist has the same perception, braking, balance, or ability to restart. Distinguish stopping at a sign from requirements at a red signal; changing one does not silently authorize the other.

Candidate responses could include retaining a common stop rule with better street design; a legally authorized and clearly communicated stop-as-yield arrangement under specified conditions; lower speeds; daylighted crossings; different intersection control; or an integrated corridor redesign. These are alternatives for domain assessment, not recommendations for a live street trial. Neighborhood preference alone cannot establish legal authority or engineering adequacy.

NHTSA's March 2022 fact sheet summarizes favorable bicycle-safety findings for stop-as-yield laws in evaluated settings and retains yielding responsibilities.[^nhtsa] That is reason to examine the underlying studies. This inquiry has not appraised them sufficiently to infer a local causal effect, evaluate every affected group, or establish present California law.

Compare options against a competent baseline. Examine serious harm, conflicts and yielding, crossing completion and waiting, journey reliability, access failures, user comprehension, enforcement burden, and displaced effects. Record the denominators: people may avoid an intimidating crossing, reducing recorded incidents while losing access. Rare serious crashes require suitable exposure data, time, and domain methods; a short period with none is not proof of safety.

The protest can motivate an inquiry into whether a procedure serves its purpose. It cannot decide which replacement is justified. Throughput is one consequence among others; neither more compliance nor faster movement alone settles the design.

## 6. Feedback must reach binding decisions

The proposed loop is:

```text
shared protections + situated knowledge + domain evidence
                         ↓
options → legitimate authorization → bounded implementation
  ↑                                      ↓
revision / repair / retirement ← consequences + objections
```

Adaptive management offers an established neighboring practice of structured decisions, monitoring, assessment, and adjustment under uncertainty.[^adaptive] The application here is an explicit translation. Feedback does not automatically produce learning or authorize a change.

Someone must be responsible for receiving criticism, judging its implications, funding a response, and changing actual practice when warranted. Observations need sources, uncertainty, reporting limits, and an avenue for dispute. A dashboard that can never change a binding rule is observation without effective revision capacity.

Review should use scheduled checkpoints and defined triggers, including newly excluded groups, unsafe conditions, maintenance failure, boundary changes, and persistent uncertainty. State what happens if monitoring becomes unavailable. Emergency powers should have narrow scope, an accountable decision-maker, expiry or renewed authorization, and later review. “Temporary” needs an operational ending.

Reversibility is multidimensional. A rule can be restored while an injury, lost livelihood, exposed identity, displaced household, or damaged ecosystem remains. A reversible pilot therefore needs a feasible fallback, resources, a responsible actor, limits on irreversible exposure, and a repair plan. The old arrangement may itself be harmful: rollback is not automatically the safest or fairest outcome.

## 7. Design systems that can revise their own rules

The object of design includes the process through which the design changes. Distinguish:

1. changing an action within an existing rule;
2. changing an operational rule;
3. reconsidering a goal, protection, or affected boundary;
4. changing who may propose, review, authorize, or appeal revisions.

These are different scopes of authority, not ranks of enlightenment. Sometimes the rule is sound and execution needs repair. Sometimes a binding rule or the revision process excludes the people who need to challenge it. The [existing organizational-learning inquiry](../intelligence-without-omniscience/learning-to-revise-the-rules.md) provides the deeper treatment; this note adds the spatial and interjurisdictional design question.

A self-revising institution needs more than an optimization routine. People need standing to initiate review, access to understandable reasons and evidence, practical ways to contest boundaries, and authority capable of making an approved revision real. Those powers require resources and protection from retaliation.

Software can detect mismatches, surface alternatives, simulate assumptions, maintain version history, or carry out a bounded delegation. Technical ability to rewrite a rule confers no authority to remove protections, expand the delegation, or determine whose interests count. Changes to the revision procedure itself should be conspicuous and separately authorized, so repeated small edits cannot quietly erase the safeguards.

## 8. Limits and tradeoffs

| Tension | Failure to look for | A question for review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local fit / common protection | Local custom legitimizes exclusion, or remote standards erase access needs | Who can challenge either authority, and obtain a practical remedy? |
| Variation / predictability | Visitors face an invisible patchwork | Can the rule be learned at the moment it matters? |
| Participation / burden | Those with time, money, confidence, or status dominate | Whose absence changes the decision, and how is participation supported? |
| Learning / stability | Constant changes undermine trust and comprehension | What change cadence, notice, and stable guarantees are needed? |
| Evidence / urgency | Unsafe inertia or premature experimentation | What action is justified now, and what uncertainty remains unacceptable? |
| Local autonomy / external effects | A locality exports harm or underprovides shared infrastructure | Which wider body can coordinate and be held accountable? |
| Measurement / lived access | A proxy improves while people withdraw or lose options | What disaggregated observations and independent objections can contradict it? |
| Adaptation / capture | Repeated exceptions entrench powerful interests | Are burdens, beneficiaries, amendments, and appeals independently inspectable? |

Some needs conflict without a satisfying compromise. Scarcity, incompatible uses, ecological limits, and historical injustice do not dissolve because governance is participatory. Refusal and exit matter, but exit can be unaffordable and does not replace voice, protection, repair, or the right to remain.

This proposal could fail because coordination costs exceed local benefits, decision capacity is insufficient, capture defeats safeguards, the wrong boundary is chosen, or an existing domain method does the job more clearly. [Comparative research](design-and-research.md#comparative-hypotheses) should be able to narrow or reject the principle's practical added value. The aim is dependable protection and workable relationships, including the option to discover that a shared rule is the better local solution.
