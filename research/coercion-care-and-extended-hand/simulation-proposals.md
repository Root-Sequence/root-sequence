# Extended Hand: Proposed Comparisons

**Status:** PUBLIC RESEARCH BRIEF / AI-assisted / unexecuted / author and methods review pending, 2026-10-26.<br>
**Scope:** Hypotheses Coherent World or another explicitly adopting project could translate. No engine, calibrated behavior, session, autonomous population, empirical finding, or project adoption is supplied by this file.<br>
**Source boundary:** New synthetic design proposals motivated by the [synthesis](synthesis.md); historical events are not reenacted. Private scenario specifications are not reproduced here.

## 1. What to compare

Use a fictional community providing food access, repair, communication support, and urgent assistance. People have differing available time, language, mobility, connectivity, skills, dependents, and resources. These are design dimensions, not predictions about real demographic groups. Include nonparticipants and people affected by decisions across the boundary.

Compare a competent centralized service, local cooperative provision, a federation, and an informal volunteer network. First hold resources, task competence, demand, and enforceable protections comparable. Later vary them deliberately. Define each arrangement through powers, information routes, appointment/removal, recourse, and resource control. Do not encode an institutional label as benevolence, aggression, intelligence, speed, or trustworthiness.

Separate **task coordination**, **persistent decision concentration**, and **coercive leverage**. A coordinator need not be a ruler. A group without officers may still make refusal costly through resource or reputational control.

## 2. Hypotheses and observations that could defeat them

These are directional hypotheses under stated conditions, not assertions of universal effects. Their measures are proposed operationalizations and require validity review.

| ID / hypothesis | Intervention and comparison | Observations | Failure or reversal to retain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| H1 — Resourced revocation reduces entrenched control when alternatives are usable | Revocable delegation plus competent replacement and portable records, compared with revocation only on paper | Time/personal cost to replace a decision-maker; concentration and persistence of vetoes; service interruption | Extra churn or skill loss worsens service and leaves recipients more dependent; no reduction in control |
| H2 — Under a fixed shortfall, discretionary allocation concentrates veto power more than a transparent allocation rule with adequately resourced challenge | Cross a common resource shortfall with allocation rules, reserves, shared threat, and initial inequality | Unmet needs by subgroup; work shifted; capture and persistence of allocation power | Discretion produces equal or less concentration; the contestable rule consumes critical resources or increases exclusion. Scarcity alone is not assigned a fixed effect |
| H3 — For the fixed service packet, request-limited signals preserve completion and hazard detection while reducing exposure relative to persistent profiles | Compare request-limited signals, purpose-limited confidential review, and persistent profiles; include a low-data baseline | Response completion and delay, missed hazards, disclosure burden, false accusations, unauthorized inference, correction cost | Minimized signals miss more consequential harm or complete fewer requests; exposure does not decrease. Report any privacy/security tradeoff separately |
| H4 — Explicit expiry and funded independent review reduce retained exceptional powers after the urgent event, at a separately measured response cost | Defined urgent scope, expiry, independent review, and restoration of ordinary control versus indefinite emergency discretion; vary enforcement and review capacity | Harm during response; false intervention; delay; actual retained powers; ability to reverse or remedy | Exceptional powers persist equally or longer; oversight is captured; response delay or unremedied harm increases. A formal expiry date is not counted as actual expiry |
| H5 — Substitute essential access and supported participation reduce loss of help after refusal compared with the same consent rule alone | Same nominal consent rule with/without substitute essential access, supported participation, and affordable departure | Cost of refusal, help retained after exit, actual feasible alternatives, who abandons a complaint | Alternative support is inaccessible, costly refusal persists, or resources diverted to it increase others' unmet essential needs |
| H6 — Unequal availability and network position concentrate decisions without malicious goals; supported challenge reduces that concentration | First vary time/expertise/network position with identical nonmalicious task goals; then compare explicit accountable roles and supported participation with informal allocation | Proposal uptake, agenda/veto concentration, excluded knowledge, workload, decisions changed after challenge | Concentration does not increase under inequality; formalization entrenches status or suppresses useful knowledge; challenge fails to reduce concentration. Test the two predictions separately |

For H3, fewer complaints cannot be read as less harm without accounting for reporting access and retaliation. For H6, identical goals do not imply identical information or equal ability to participate. For all comparisons, an objection heard is not necessarily an objection remedied.

## 3. Three bounded scenario cards

### A. The well-meaning bottleneck

One skilled repair maintainer gradually becomes the route for equipment, records, and decisions. Nobody seeks dominance. Introduce illness or absence, a disputed repair priority, and one person who cannot attend the usual meeting. Test H1 and H6 separately, then their interaction. Disclose whether replacement competence exists: a missing expert cannot be replaced by declaring a vote.

### B. The shortage and the offered hand

A supply interruption reduces available assistance while needs differ. Someone declines the standard service because its conditions are unacceptable. Compare access through an alternative, a costly appeal, and no practical alternative; compare transparent rationing with discretionary allocation. Test H2 and H5. Preserve the case where helpers must refuse additional work, and where protection for one person reduces resources for another.

### C. The eye that might become a camera

An ambiguous safety incident prompts a demand to collect everyone's histories. Compare request-level coordination, a confidential scoped investigation, and persistent profiles. Introduce a false allegation, an actual unmet urgent need, a provider exploiting privacy to hide error, and a request to extend emergency powers. Test H3 and H4 without assuming either maximal disclosure or minimal data always wins.

## 4. Protocol before execution

1. Freeze a versioned fixture: actors and nonusers, initial inequalities, skills, stocks and flows, necessary tasks, rights/authority cards, information access, shock schedule, stopping conditions, and transition rules. Choose enough detail for a meaningful decision; do not let narration fill a missing rule.
2. Specify numerical parameters and measurement definitions before comparing outcomes. This brief deliberately does not pretend that empirically grounded costs or behavioral probabilities are already available. No run starts until they are recorded and reviewed.
3. Use matched starting states and shock sequences. Change one mechanism first; then examine interactions. In stochastic implementations, use paired random seeds and predeclare repetitions. In paper work, retain alternative rule branches and facilitation choices rather than inventing statistical significance.
4. Include negative controls: an arrangement name changed with identical rules should not change transitions; unreachable appeal authority should not count as effective recourse; hidden extra labor must not rescue a favorite design. Use an identical competent baseline before testing different capacities.
5. Vary the initial distribution of resources, centrality, expertise, and access independently of the institutional label. Separate transition costs from settled operation. Test maintenance absence, ordinary mistakes, and conflicting needs before adding deliberate malicious behavior.
6. Keep a ledger: state before/after, actor, action, actual authority, permission, information available, resources consumed, burden shifted, observed harm, repair, assumption ID, and unresolved uncertainty. Preserve a branch on breach or redesign; never overwrite the failure.

## 5. Report plural outcomes

Report security and harm, access and unmet need, refusal/exit costs, decision concentration, privacy exposure, uptake of distributed knowledge, maintenance burden, and recovery separately, by affected role and access condition. Distinguish objective task state from what each actor can know. No universal liberty, care, intelligence, or coherence score may erase concentrated harm.

Stop or mark a branch uninterpretable if it needs unrecorded authority, an invented causal rule, free extra work, or an unavailable perspective. Human playtesting would need its own accessible briefing, voluntary participation, and review. It would provide feedback on that encounter, not representative evidence about a political system.

Permissible model claim: under specified costs and permissions, an appeal consumed resources and changed a decision. Impermissible jump: anarchism, the state, intelligence, abundance, or scarcity necessarily produces a particular moral outcome.
