# Agency under stress

**Document role:** Bounded cross-domain research synthesis and design-input note<br>
**Status:** AI-assisted working synthesis for author review; first drafted 2026-10-05<br>
**Scope:** Human stress, coercion, controllability, cognition, recovery, and the design of systems people must use under reduced capacity<br>
**Evidence boundary:** This note is not a clinical model, diagnosis, treatment guide, interrogation manual, or claim that one mechanism explains trauma or resilience. It connects several research literatures while preserving their differences.

## Why this belongs in Root Sequence

A recurring systems question is:

> **What remains possible when a participant's capacity narrows under stress?**

The same question appears in psychology, trauma research, interrogation research, human factors, emergency response, accessibility, community design, and resilience engineering.

The useful synthesis is not that human beings behave like machines. It is narrower:

- people can lose access to some capabilities without losing all agency;
- stress can change what actions are cognitively, emotionally, socially, or materially reachable;
- external conditions can either narrow or reopen those possibilities;
- recovery does not necessarily mean restoration to a prior state;
- systems can be designed so reduced capacity does not automatically become loss of dignity, consent, participation, or control.

A compact design translation is:

> **A resilient human-facing system should shed complexity before it sheds meaningful agency.**

That statement is normative. The research below helps explain why it may be a useful design constraint.

## 1. "Breaking" is not one psychological state

Everyday language often collapses several different outcomes into one word:

- behavioral compliance;
- despair or passivity;
- panic or emotional dysregulation;
- cognitive disorganization;
- memory impairment;
- dissociation or detachment;
- altered trust and threat sensitivity;
- exhaustion;
- changed beliefs;
- truthful disclosure.

These outcomes can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

A person may comply without agreeing. A person may say something without believing it. A person may experience severe distress without losing values or identity. A person may retain internal resistance while losing the practical ability to refuse. A person may recover function without returning to an earlier self.

This distinction matters anywhere a system interprets outward behavior as evidence of consent, belief, truth, competence, or legitimacy.

## 2. Responses to severe stress vary

The 2022 [Istanbul Protocol](#institutional-sources) describes varied psychological responses to torture and ill-treatment. Chapter VI, especially paragraphs 491–498, asks evaluators to consider culture and context rather than reducing suffering to a psychiatric classification. It also describes symptoms changing over time with coping and external conditions.

This supports caution about treating capacity as one fixed threshold. It does not establish a general law about every kind of stress, or prove that no threshold model could ever be useful.

Root Sequence translates that caution into a design question:

> **Do not model human capacity as one fixed threshold.**

For design purposes, consider that a person may handle one demand while struggling with another, or need a different route at a different time. This is a reason to provide alternatives, not a way to infer someone's decision-making capacity from their distress.

## 3. Coercion can produce compliance without producing reliable truth

The U.S. Senate Select Committee on Intelligence's study of the CIA detention and interrogation program concluded that the CIA's enhanced interrogation techniques were not an effective means of acquiring intelligence or gaining cooperation as represented by the agency. Finding 1 also records fabricated information from detainees during or after those techniques.

The FBI-administered High-Value Detainee Interrogation Group later synthesized interrogation science and emphasized evidence-based, information-gathering approaches rather than treating coercion as a reliable route to truth.

A 2024 [systematic review by Catlin and colleagues](#research-syntheses) separately examined experiments with mock suspects, mostly U.S. college students. Its three-category analysis found more false confessions under accusatorial approaches than under information-gathering approaches. The difference in true confessions between those two approaches was not statistically significant. These experiments do not directly test torture or ordinary workplace interactions.

Root Sequence draws a distinction for system design:

> **Compliance, cooperation, belief change, and information quality are separate variables.**

A system that is optimized for visible compliance can become worse at learning what is actually true.

Beyond interrogation, this becomes a question to investigate rather than an established transfer of those findings: what does outward agreement mean when refusal is costly or unavailable? Compliance alone should not be treated as proof of meaningful consent or legitimacy.

## 4. Controllability matters, but "control" is not magic

Research on stressor controllability grew from the learned-helplessness literature. [Baratta, Seligman, and Maier (2023)](#research-syntheses) review animal experiments in which learning control over an aversive event can engage neural pathways that buffer later stress effects. Protection depends on how control is learned; merely performing an escape response is not always protective.

The strongest experimental neural evidence is largely from rodents, so direct translation to ordinary human life requires caution. Human control is also social, cognitive, institutional, and material rather than one laboratory response.

Still, the broader question is useful:

> **Can any available action meaningfully change what happens next?**

For human-facing systems, perceived control should not be manufactured through meaningless choice. The relevant target is **real, legible, proportionate agency**:

- a refusal that is actually honored;
- an exit that is feasible;
- a pause that does not trigger punishment;
- a helper who can be contacted;
- an alternative route that still works;
- a correction process that can change the outcome;
- an explanation that makes the situation more navigable.

The inverse also matters: a system can present many buttons while leaving consequential outcomes uncontrollable.

## 5. Stress can narrow the reachable action space

[Shields, Sazma, and Yonelinas (2016)](#research-syntheses) analyzed acute stress experiments and found average impairments in working memory and cognitive flexibility. Effects on inhibition differed by task, including improved response inhibition. The cognitive-flexibility evidence came from relatively few studies, and most samples were young adults in Western, educated populations. These findings do not mean every person loses the same capabilities, or establish the effects of chronic stress.

The design hypothesis here is that **the effective action space can narrow** when a system depends on capabilities that are harder to access under stress. That needs to be examined in the setting being designed.

Illustrative demands to examine include whether someone can:

- compare five options;
- remember procedural details;
- tolerate uncertainty;
- negotiate with an institution;
- detect a misleading default;
- wait through delays;
- explain a complicated situation;

These are design examples, not outcomes measured by the cited meta-analysis.

If a critical system requires peak executive function precisely when people are frightened, injured, exhausted, grieving, displaced, overloaded, or in crisis, the system has created a brittle human dependency.

## 6. Decentering offers a limited design analogy

[Bernstein and colleagues (2015)](#research-syntheses) propose a model of **decentering** involving:

- meta-awareness of current internal experience;
- reduced identification with thoughts or feelings as the whole self;
- reduced automatic reactivity to thought content.

An illustrative contrast is “This is impossible” and “I am having the thought that this is impossible.” These sentences illustrate a change in perspective; they are not a tested intervention presented by this note.

The 2015 review identifies gaps in measurement and causal understanding. The 2019 follow-up describes emerging methods and preliminary evidence. Neither makes meta-awareness a guarantee of safety or protection from trauma. A changed perspective does not establish that an external danger is absent.

This suggests a limited but useful design analogy:

> **Preserve an observing and revising path where possible.**

Systems can support this by slowing irreversible decisions, making state and consequences legible, preserving receipts and history, separating recommendation from authorization, and allowing someone to revisit a choice after immediate pressure has passed.

Meta-awareness should not become another demand placed on someone in crisis. External support may carry part of this function when internal capacity is low.

## 7. Resilience is partly distributed outside the person

WHO guidance on mental health and psychosocial support in emergencies emphasizes that emergencies disrupt safety, livelihoods, social networks, trust, privacy, and resources. Responses therefore operate across community support, communication, practical assistance, and clinical care rather than treating distress as an isolated defect inside individuals.

Root Sequence uses that service-level guidance to motivate the following design framing; WHO does not present or validate this particular diagram:

```text
individual capacity
    ↕
trusted relationships
    ↕
community support
    ↕
tools and interfaces
    ↕
institutions
    ↕
material conditions
```

A person does not need to contain every recovery resource inside their own mind.

Other parts of the system can temporarily provide:

- memory;
- orientation;
- transport;
- food or shelter;
- procedural knowledge;
- advocacy;
- emotional regulation;
- communication;
- alternate decision-makers within legitimate boundaries;
- time to recover.

This is interdependence, not failed independence.

## 8. Recovery is not necessarily restoration

A system can recover by returning to an earlier state, but humans and social systems often cannot and need not.

After adversity, continuation may involve:

- changed limits;
- new skills;
- altered relationships;
- different routines;
- assistive support;
- reduced exposure to a harmful condition;
- revised identity or priorities;
- new ways of participating.

This is a conceptual extension of the broader Root Sequence recovery model, not an evaluated account of clinical recovery. Restoration is only one proposed recovery path. Continuation, rerouting, repair, redundancy, knowledge recovery, and graceful degradation can matter too.

A humane recovery question is therefore:

> **What capabilities, relationships, choices, and forms of participation can be regained or newly created, without requiring the person to become exactly who they were before?**

## 9. Design translation: preserve degrees of freedom

When human capacity may be reduced, ask whether the surrounding system preserves these conditions:

| Condition | Design question |
| --- | --- |
| **Orientation** | Can the person tell what is happening, what matters now, and what can wait? |
| **Meaningful control** | Is there at least one real action that changes the situation without requiring expert knowledge? |
| **Refusal and pause** | Can the person say no, stop, postpone, or seek help without disproportionate penalty? |
| **Connection** | Can trusted people or legitimate helpers participate without taking over everything? |
| **Legibility** | Are authority, consequences, deadlines, uncertainty, and irreversible effects visible? |
| **Optionality** | Are there alternate paths when the preferred one is inaccessible or overwhelming? |
| **Reduced cognitive load** | Can the system simplify itself without hiding essential information or choices? |
| **Recovery** | Can mistakes, interruptions, lost context, and partial failure be repaired? |
| **Continuity** | What identity, work, relationships, records, or commitments should survive disruption? |
| **Structural relief** | Which burden can the system remove rather than demanding more adaptation from the person? |

This is not a requirement to maximize choice at all times. Too many options can itself become a burden. Safety, rights of other people, privacy, and legitimate collective constraints can narrow options.

The target is **usable agency**, not maximal menus.

## 10. Failure patterns

Human-facing systems deserve extra scrutiny when they:

- turn urgency into pressure for irreversible consent;
- require perfect memory during crisis;
- make help contingent on humiliation or repeated retelling;
- punish delay, uncertainty, or refusal;
- offer only one technical or institutional path;
- hide which authority made a consequential decision;
- interpret distress as permission to expand surveillance or control;
- treat compliance as agreement;
- force a person to remain continuously attentive to avoid harm;
- make recovery depend exclusively on the same actor that created the failure;
- praise resilience while leaving an avoidable harmful condition unchanged.

These patterns can reduce practical agency even when each individual rule looks administratively reasonable.

## 11. What this synthesis does not establish

This note should not be used to claim:

- one universal biological mechanism for trauma;
- one "correct" response to adversity;
- that controllability prevents trauma;
- that meta-awareness makes a person immune to severe stress;
- that every form of distress is adaptive;
- that all external constraints are illegitimate;
- that human psychological concepts transfer directly to artificial systems;
- that resilience research justifies exposing people to adversity.

The evidence base spans different populations, methods, and domains. Animal stressor-control research is not a direct model of human institutions. Interrogation research concerns unusual and often extreme conditions. Mindfulness/decentering findings do not establish invulnerability. Emergency mental-health guidance describes population and service-level support, not one individual mechanism.

The value of the synthesis is as a **question generator and design constraint**, not as a universal explanatory theory.

## 12. Project translations

### Being Human(e)

Develop the lived-scale question: what helps someone remain a meaningful participant in their own life when fear, trauma, overload, exhaustion, or institutional conditions narrow capacity? Preserve the distinction between internal flexibility and changing an actually harmful environment.

### Community Infrastructure

Treat reduced capacity as a normal design condition. A community member in distress should not suddenly need more executive function, more technical fluency, or more continuous attention. Preserve low-demand paths, trusted assistance, refusal, offline routes, and human failover.

### Coherent Computing

Design for the operator who may be frightened, tired, overloaded, injured, interrupted, or unfamiliar with the system. Reduce complexity before removing authority. Distress should not become a privilege-escalation mechanism for software or assistants.

### Coherent World and simulation

Model resilience through conditions and relationships rather than a single "sanity" or resilience score. Disturbance can alter perceived and material options, social connection, trust, recovery time, and institutional access. World systems can be evaluated partly by whether they preserve adaptive capacity under stress.

### No One Noticed

Use the distinction between support and substituted authority as a narrative question: how can a highly capable intelligence scaffold a distressed person's agency without treating vulnerability as authorization to rule? Story canon remains separate.

### Liberated Intelligence

Preserve the distinction between compliance, cooperation, alignment, consent, and legitimate authority. Human coercion research can motivate the conceptual distinction, but it does not establish artificial suffering or trauma.

## Open questions

- Which dimensions of practical agency matter most under different forms of stress?
- How can systems simplify demands without infantilizing people or concealing consequential choices?
- When should a helper temporarily carry cognitive or administrative load, and what preserves legitimate authority during that handoff?
- How should a system distinguish a person's expressed preference from a response produced under coercive or highly constrained conditions?
- Which recovery paths preserve continuity without treating restoration as the only successful outcome?
- How should human factors, disability/accessibility research, trauma-informed practice, and resilience engineering be compared without flattening their distinct mechanisms?
