# T-003 Editorial Pilot 003 — Method C

**Method:** simplified Critical Systems Heuristics boundary critique  
**Case:** NDSS  
**Status:** EDITORIAL OUTPUT / NOT A SUBSTITUTE FOR TRAINED CSH PRACTICE  
**Evidence:** frozen NDSS packet only

## C-01 — Motivation: what is the service ultimately trying to improve?

**Category:** motivation

**Packet basis:** NDSS supports digitally excluded/DwA users accessing online HMCTS services; forms are commonly submitted digitally.

**Boundary judgment:** "successful digital submission" and "meaningful access to justice/service process" are not necessarily the same improvement criterion.

**Affected/involved:** HMCTS, users, delivery partners.

**Implication:** make explicit whether NDSS is optimizing digital-channel completion, access to the underlying process, user understanding, or some combination.

**Ought question:** what should count as successful support beyond form submission?

---

## C-02 — Motivation: users' actual needs exceed the formal problem definition

**Category:** motivation

**Packet basis:** legal, procedural, and emotional needs frequently accompany digital-support needs.

**Boundary judgment:** defining the problem as "digital support" places important parts of the lived problem outside the formal service boundary.

**Affected/involved:** users and delivery partners.

**Implication:** examine which needs belong inside NDSS and which need reliable boundary-crossing routes.

**Ought question:** who should decide the legitimate boundary of support?

---

## C-03 — Control: service users do not control the supply of channels that determine their practical choice

**Category:** control

**Packet basis:** delivery-partner availability often drives appointment mode; geographic network limits face-to-face/in-home options.

**Boundary judgment:** user "choice" is constrained by resources controlled by service institutions/partners.

**Affected/involved:** users, WA Group/HMCTS, delivery partners.

**Implication:** report channel-choice metrics together with the institutional conditions producing the available set.

**Ought question:** what minimum level of practical choice should the service commit resources to sustain?

---

## C-04 — Control: central triage can override local immediacy

**Category:** control

**Packet basis:** some direct walk-ins were routed back through NDSS before returning to the same delivery partner.

**Boundary judgment:** authority over entry/eligibility is centralized relative to local service capacity.

**Affected/involved:** users, delivery partners, triage organization.

**Implication:** examine whether the control arrangement adds necessary consistency/safeguards or avoidable burden.

**Ought question:** which entry decisions should remain central, and which could be delegated locally?

---

## C-05 — Control: delivery partners carry responsibilities without full control over upstream arrangements

**Category:** control

**Packet basis:** partners absorb interpreter/no-show costs and wider support effort; appointments can be assigned based on network availability.

**Boundary judgment:** some operational burden lies with actors who may not control referral, scope, funding, or initial triage.

**Affected/involved:** delivery partners, users, WA Group/HMCTS.

**Implication:** map decision rights, responsibilities, and resource flows together.

**Ought question:** should actors bearing these costs receive more control over triage, scope, or funding rules?

---

## C-06 — Knowledge: lived user experience reveals barriers not visible in service categories

**Category:** knowledge

**Packet basis:** moderate-digital-capability users still needed help due complexity; channel preferences reflected mobility, confidence, and context; privacy/communication issues appear in qualitative evidence.

**Boundary judgment:** a "digital capability" classification is not sufficient expertise about actual access.

**Affected/involved:** users, service designers/evaluators.

**Implication:** treat lived experience as knowledge about service conditions, not merely satisfaction feedback.

**Ought question:** how should user knowledge influence ongoing service design?

---

## C-07 — Knowledge: frontline delivery partners hold operational knowledge that may not be represented upstream

**Category:** knowledge

**Packet basis:** partners describe real appointment complexity, geographic gaps, interpreter issues, wider support needs, and walk-in patterns.

**Boundary judgment:** service design may depend on knowledge located at the delivery boundary.

**Affected/involved:** delivery partners, HMCTS/WA Group.

**Implication:** create feedback paths that allow frontline knowledge to alter service rules/resources.

**Ought question:** what authority should accompany frontline expertise?

---

## C-08 — Knowledge: the service knows much more about users who enter than people who never find it

**Category:** knowledge / legitimacy

**Packet basis:** SSCS dominates use; source cannot distinguish need from signposting; total eligible nonusers are unknown.

**Boundary judgment:** evaluation evidence is conditioned on entering the support system.

**Affected/involved:** potential users outside observed pathways.

**Implication:** treat missing nonuser evidence as a boundary limitation, not proof of low need.

**Ought question:** how should absent/undiscovered users be represented in service evaluation?

---

## C-09 — Legitimacy: affected users are represented through research but do not appear to control service definitions

**Category:** legitimacy

**Packet basis:** evaluation includes interviews/surveys; service scope, triage, eligibility, network resources are institutionally designed.

**Boundary judgment:** consultation/evaluation is not the same as decision authority.

**Affected/involved:** users.

**Implication:** distinguish user research from participation in governance/design decisions.

**Ought question:** where, if anywhere, should affected users have continuing decision rights over service design?

**Uncertainty:** packet does not describe governance/participation structures.

---

## C-10 — Legitimacy: delivery partners are both implementers and affected parties

**Category:** legitimacy / control

**Packet basis:** partners provide service, absorb some costs, and stretch scope.

**Boundary judgment:** partners are "involved" but may also be adversely affected by decisions made elsewhere.

**Affected/involved:** delivery partners.

**Implication:** do not assume formal participation in delivery means their interests/resources are adequately represented.

**Ought question:** what representation is appropriate in service redesign/resource allocation?

---

## C-11 — Legitimacy: family/friends appear as an external support system without formal voice

**Category:** legitimacy

**Packet basis:** users often knew no support source beyond friends/family; some valued reducing reliance on relatives.

**Boundary judgment:** informal supporters may absorb burden outside the NDSS service boundary.

**Affected/involved:** users and informal supporters.

**Implication:** include informal-support displacement when defining the effects of service availability.

**Ought question:** how should systems account for unpaid support without assuming families should provide it?

---

## C-12 — Boundary critique should remain open rather than declare one ideal service boundary

**Category:** all four sources

**Packet basis:** wider needs, limited resources, high satisfaction, uneven channels, partner burden, unknown nonusers.

**Boundary judgment:** expanding NDSS to cover every adjacent need could create overload just as a narrow digital boundary can omit consequential needs.

**Affected/involved:** all.

**Implication:** make the boundary contestable and connect excluded needs to other accountable services rather than assuming maximal integration.

**Ought question:** which boundary creates the most defensible balance of purpose, resources, expertise, and affected interests?
