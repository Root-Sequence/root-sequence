## Decision question

> **Given only this packet, what changes to NDSS service design and delivery should be prioritized to improve eligible users' practical ability to access and complete online HMCTS processes?**

---

## 1. Service background

Between 2016 and 2025, HMCTS ran a reform programme that moved a substantial number of services online.

The NDSS was established in recognition that some public users were:

- **Digitally Excluded (DE)**; or
- **Digital with Assistance (DwA)** — able to access digital services only with assistance.

The NDSS was intended to support access to HMCTS online services.

The evaluation ran from July 2022 to July 2025 while the service was being rolled out.

---

## 2. Evaluation design

The final report states that the evaluation included:

- 50 qualitative interviews with service users;
- a survey of 534 service users;
- 45 qualitative interviews with HMCTS, Courts and Tribunals Service Centre, WA Group, and delivery-partner staff;
- a survey of 35 WA Group/delivery-partner staff;
- 5 case-study appointment observations;
- analysis of management information.

The evaluation was conducted in three phases so emerging findings could inform rollout.

This packet does not independently assess sampling bias, response bias, interview coding, or representativeness beyond the report's own scope.

---

## 3. Typical service journey

The accessible summary describes a typical journey as:

1. referral or self-referral;
2. triage by We Are Group to establish eligibility and need for digital support;
3. booking with an appropriate delivery partner;
4. appointment;
5. digital support delivered;
6. follow-up appointments where required.

Referrals could come from Courts and Tribunals Service Centres or delivery partners, as well as self-referral.

---

## 4. Scale and distribution

From November 2022 to January 2025:

- 10,214 individuals were triaged;
- 10,419 cases were recorded;
- 9,005 individual users were supported during an appointment.

The accessible summary reports:

- 90% of triaged cases concerned Social Security and Child Support (SSCS) services;
- 84% of closed appointments were marked as having provided support;
  - 73% fully supported;
  - 11% partially supported.

Appointment demand was not evenly distributed across the delivery-partner network.

The full report states that 35% of 136 delivery partners had fewer than 10 sessions scheduled over roughly two years.

The report says a large network is necessary for face-to-face support and describes the network as not large enough to make that option viable for all users.

---

## 5. Aggregated user profile

The accessible summary reports among evaluated service users:

- 46% were aged 55 or older;
- 89% had a health condition lasting more than 12 months;
- 82% were not in paid work;
- 43% reported annual income below £14,000;
- 37% had no formal qualifications.

The full report says health/disability issues including motor, learning, and cognitive difficulties commonly contributed to digital exclusion or need for assistance.

These are aggregate characteristics from the evaluated population.

Do not infer any individual user's condition from them.

---

## 6. Digital capability was not the whole need

The accessible summary reports that only 20% of service users were classified as having low digital capability.

Others had at least moderate digital capability but sought support because of:

- concern about the complexity of interacting with HMCTS services;
- uncertainty about how to progress an appeal, claim, or application;
- need for reassurance.

The full report states that only 29% had tried to use the online form themselves before contacting NDSS.

It reports that most interviewed users were unaware of other potential sources of support beyond friends or family.

---

## 7. Discoverability and referral

Users most often believed they heard about NDSS through HMCTS or DWP.

The report describes uncertainty about how some users were actually routed to NDSS.

It notes that SSCS users had more established pathways through DWP → CTSC → NDSS than users of other services.

The evaluation says support was heavily skewed toward SSCS users and that available evidence could not determine whether this reflected:

- higher underlying need; or
- better signposting/referral for SSCS users.

Some users interviewed were surprised and relieved that the support existed.

---

## 8. Support needs exceeded the service's nominal label

At triage:

- 85% reported seeking digital support;
- 74% also reported seeking legal support;
- 69% procedural support;
- 43% emotional support.

Delivery partners said users often expected help beyond digital support.

The report says delivery partners frequently provided wider support in order to meet users' actual needs.

This packet does not decide whether those forms of support should formally belong inside NDSS.

---

## 9. Channel choice and practical availability

In the service-user survey:

- 51% recalled being offered a choice between in-person and remote support;
- 29% recalled not being offered that choice;
- 20% were unsure.

The same figure reports:

- 86% said they received support in their preferred way;
- 9% said they did not;
- 5% did not know.

Reported appointment modes were overwhelmingly remote.

Users described reasons for preferring remote support including:

- physical health/mobility making travel difficult;
- mental-health/social reasons;
- wanting the quickest available appointment.

Other users preferred face-to-face support because:

- they lacked confidence on the phone;
- their health/access needs made in-person help preferable;
- they wanted more personal interaction.

The report describes cases where the *nominal* availability of face-to-face support did not make it a realistic option:

- some locations were too far away;
- examples included roughly two-hour bus journeys;
- waits could be two weeks or more;
- in-home appointments were unavailable in some areas;
- staff said booking was often driven by partner availability, not user need.

The report says the delivery-partner network was not large enough to meet all face-to-face preference.

---

## 10. Walk-in and handoff friction

The report describes cases where users walked directly into a delivery-partner location seeking help.

Rather than receiving support directly, they could be referred back to the NDSS process and later booked into an appointment with that same delivery partner.

The report characterizes this as an integration/efficiency problem.

The packet contains no reliable estimate of how many appointments followed this path.

---

## 11. Appointment support

Accessible-summary results include:

- 94% of users needing help accessing the form received this help;
- 84% needing advice on case content received this help;
- 72% needing emotional support received this help.

Online forms were submitted during appointments for 75% of service users.

The packet does not establish whether adviser-completed submission versus user-completed submission produces different longer-term user capability.

---

## 12. Privacy, sensory access, communication, and local conditions

The full report includes reports from some users of:

- poor audio quality;
- distracting/unprofessional background noise during remote calls;
- discomfort discussing sensitive/private information when advisers appeared to be in non-private settings;
- face-to-face locations lacking privacy;
- delays or disrupted appointments;
- old or poor equipment;
- communication difficulties involving accents/language.

The report also identifies a need for stronger delivery-partner support/training around users with sensory disabilities.

The packet does not quantify prevalence of each issue beyond the report's qualitative wording where percentages are absent.

---

## 13. Delivery-partner burden and resource mismatch

The report describes delivery partners sometimes absorbing costs or effort not fully aligned with the service model.

Examples include:

- arranging staff for appointments that users did not attend;
- interpreter cost in some cases where an interpreter had not been arranged upstream;
- providing broader procedural/legal/emotional help than the original digital-support framing;
- appointment time pressures for complex needs.

This packet does not include a complete cost model or fee structure.

---

## 14. Outcomes

The accessible summary reports:

- 80% said NDSS made completing their form easier;
- 18% said they would not have submitted the form at all without NDSS support;
- 19% said they would only have been able to submit on paper without the service.

Overall satisfaction:

- 86% satisfied;
- 5% dissatisfied;
- 68% said support matched or exceeded expectations.

The packet does not establish causal effects on eventual legal/case outcomes.

---

## 15. Follow-up and continuity of support

The full report states:

- 15% reported further contact with an adviser after the initial appointment;
- 80% reported no further contact;
- 77% felt very/fairly clear about next steps after support ended;
- 20% were not very/not at all clear.

Some users valued follow-up communications.

Some were unsure which organization—delivery partner, DWP, HMCTS—had sent later communications.

Some users in more complex/non-SSCS services reported needing multiple appointments or still being unclear on next steps.

---

## 16. Important uncertainties

The packet does not provide enough evidence to determine:

- the total eligible population who never discovered NDSS;
- whether SSCS/non-SSCS take-up differences are primarily need or referral differences;
- the counterfactual submission/completion rate without NDSS for the full eligible population;
- the causal effect of face-to-face versus remote support;
- which channel preferences would change if travel/wait constraints disappeared;
- the best service boundary between digital, procedural, legal, and emotional support;
- full delivery-partner costs;
- geographic coverage metrics sufficient to optimize the network;
- whether adviser-led form completion increases or reduces future independent capability;
- the distribution of outcomes by disability type or access need beyond reported summaries;
- legal adequacy of any service design;
- post-2025 implementation changes.

Methods should request missing evidence rather than fill these gaps.
