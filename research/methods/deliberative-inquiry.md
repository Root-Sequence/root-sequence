# Deliberative Inquiry

**Document role:** shared Root Sequence research and decision method  
**Status:** working / review-pending  
**Scope:** collective learning, judgment, and consequential decisions  
**Not:** a universal voting rule, a demand for unanimity, or a substitute for expertise

## Core idea

**Deliberative inquiry** is a process in which participants build sufficient shared understanding before making a consequential collective judgment or authorizing action.

Its goal is not to make everyone agree.

Its goal is to improve the conditions under which people can:

- understand the question and consequences;
- examine evidence and competing explanations;
- ask for missing information;
- hear affected perspectives;
- revise their own models;
- identify what remains uncertain or value-dependent;
- decide what action is justified despite incomplete knowledge.

A compact distinction:

> **Debate asks who can defend a position. Deliberation asks what the group should believe or do after examining the problem together.**

Debate can still be useful inside deliberation. Adversarial challenge can expose weak reasoning. It becomes a problem when winning the encounter replaces learning.

## Bounded inquiry

“As informed as humanly possible” is a useful aspiration but an impossible stopping rule.

Deliberative inquiry therefore aims for **sufficient understanding for this decision**, scaled to:

- stakes;
- reversibility;
- urgency;
- uncertainty;
- affected populations;
- information cost;
- consequences of delay.

Low-stakes reversible choices should not require a constitutional convention. Irreversible or coercive actions deserve substantially stronger justification.

## Process

### 1. Define the decision and its boundary

Clarify:

- what is actually being decided;
- who has authority to decide it;
- who is affected;
- what remains outside scope;
- what happens if no decision is made;
- what parts are reversible.

### 2. Preserve independent first impressions where useful

Before visible camps form, participants may privately record:

- current view;
- confidence;
- assumptions;
- questions;
- what could change their mind.

This is a baseline for learning, not a commitment participants must defend.

### 3. Build a shared factual baseline

Identify facts that appear established, claims that remain contested, and missing evidence.

Use [Epistemic Contrast](epistemic-contrast.md) so the baseline does not silently inherit one group's framing.

### 4. Let participants request information

Participants should be able to surface:

- unanswered questions;
- missing expertise;
- affected perspectives not yet represented;
- alternative explanations;
- evidence they believe is being overweighted or ignored.

Experts inform the process; they do not automatically become the decision authority.

### 5. Separate kinds of disagreement

Label disagreement where possible:

- factual;
- causal;
- definitional;
- probabilistic;
- ethical / value-based;
- distributional (“who bears the cost?”);
- procedural;
- authority / legitimacy.

Some disagreements can be resolved with evidence. Others cannot, and pretending otherwise creates false consensus.

### 6. Deliberate without making domination the method

Depending on context, useful safeguards include:

- clarification before rebuttal;
- round-robin or asynchronous participation;
- anonymous issue collection;
- source-linked summaries;
- explicit alternative hypotheses;
- breaks and time limits;
- accessible formats;
- private reassessment;
- facilitation that can itself be challenged.

### 7. Reassess privately where useful

Changing one's mind should not require a public performance of defeat.

Participants may record why a judgment changed:

- new evidence;
- corrected misunderstanding;
- better explanation;
- changed value judgment;
- previously hidden consequence;
- social pressure, fatigue, or intimidation.

The last category is a process-integrity signal, not evidence for the substantive conclusion.

### 8. Preserve unresolved uncertainty and dissent

A decision record can distinguish:

- what the group concluded;
- how confident it is;
- serious unresolved objections;
- affected-party concerns;
- what evidence would reopen the question;
- review or sunset conditions.

The group can act without pretending the disagreement disappeared.

### 9. Scale authorization to consequence

A working Root Sequence principle is:

> **The more coercive, harmful, irreversible, exclusionary, privacy-invasive, or authority-concentrating an action is, the stronger the justification required to authorize it.**

This does not prescribe one threshold. It says that decision architecture should respond to what an action can do to people and systems.

### 10. Observe consequences and revise

A decision is not the end of inquiry.

Where possible, record:

- expected outcomes;
- indicators of failure;
- affected-party feedback;
- review date;
- conditions for reversal;
- newly discovered consequences.

Then feed those observations back into the model.

## Consensus is not the target

A group can be healthy and coherent while retaining disagreement.

Useful outcomes include:

- genuine convergence;
- narrower disagreement;
- clearer uncertainty;
- identification of a value conflict;
- recognition that more evidence is needed;
- a reversible experiment;
- an authorized decision with dissent preserved;
- a decision not to act.

Manufactured unanimity is not a superior outcome to accurately represented plurality.

See [Collective Judgment and Manufactured Consensus](../../analysis/collective-judgment-and-manufactured-consensus.md).

## AI boundary

AI systems may help with:

- source retrieval;
- argument mapping;
- question deduplication;
- translation and accessibility;
- comparing proposal versions;
- identifying unresolved assumptions;
- drafting source-linked summaries;
- preserving decision memory for human review.

They should not silently become the authority by:

- deciding which concerns are reasonable;
- ranking people;
- suppressing minority positions;
- inferring consent;
- replacing affected-party participation;
- presenting one generated synthesis as the group's own judgment.

AI can increase deliberative capacity. **Capability does not confer legitimacy.**

## Decision record

A proportional record might include:

```text
Question / decision
Scope
Authority
Affected parties
Evidence considered
Competing explanations
Known uncertainty
Major reasons
Unresolved dissent
Conflicts of interest
Decision rule
Reversibility
Expected consequences
Review / sunset conditions
What would reopen the question
```

Not every decision needs every field. Privacy, data minimization, and the right not to create permanent reputation records still apply.

## Working orientations

> **Deliberate before consequential decisions.**

> **Disagreement is information, not failure.**

> **Changing your mind can be evidence that the process worked.**

> **Reality gets veto power over the model.**

These are prompts for inquiry, not sacred slogans. Any of them can be challenged if they stop helping Root Sequence detect error or preserve agency.
