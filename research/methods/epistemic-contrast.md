# Epistemic Contrast

**Document role:** shared Root Sequence research method  
**Status:** working / review-pending  
**Scope:** inquiry, source comparison, model-building, and cross-project research  
**Not:** a requirement to give every claim equal weight or manufacture ideological symmetry

## Core idea

**Epistemic contrast** deliberately places accounts from different positions within or around a system beside one another so that assumptions, mechanisms, incentives, disagreements, convergences, blind spots, and missing evidence become easier to see.

It is not the same as debate.

Debate often asks:

> Which position can defeat the other?

Epistemic contrast asks:

> What becomes visible when these differently situated accounts are examined together, and which model best survives contact with the evidence?

A critic, architect, owner, worker, affected person, researcher, regulator, journalist, or observer may describe the same system differently. Those differences are useful data, but **difference itself is not evidence that every account is equally well supported**.

## Why use contrast?

Public discourse often collapses disagreement into camps. That can hide several important possibilities:

- opposing parties may agree about a mechanism while disagreeing about whether it is desirable;
- people with different incentives may reveal different parts of the same system;
- advocates may disclose assumptions that critics infer from the outside;
- critics may identify consequences that designers did not include in the system boundary;
- affected people may reveal costs invisible to owners, institutions, or aggregate metrics;
- two apparently opposed explanations may both be incomplete;
- a disagreement that looks ideological may actually be empirical, definitional, causal, or normative.

The method therefore separates **descriptive agreement**, **empirical disagreement**, **causal disagreement**, and **normative disagreement** before trying to resolve them.

## Source roles

A source can serve more than one role. Useful roles include:

- system critic;
- system advocate;
- architect / designer / decision-maker;
- owner / investor / beneficiary;
- worker / participant;
- affected person;
- researcher;
- journalist / investigator;
- regulator / auditor;
- observer;
- satirist or cultural critic.

The role does not determine truth. It helps explain **what the source can directly know, what incentives or limits may shape the account, and what additional evidence is needed**.

## Evidence roles

Useful evidence roles include:

- first-person worldview or stated intention;
- direct observation;
- primary records;
- quantitative data;
- historical case;
- experimental or quasi-experimental evidence;
- investigative reporting;
- lived experience;
- argument or interpretation;
- speculative model;
- satire / cultural diagnosis.

A billionaire explaining a desired future, a worker describing a workplace, and a peer-reviewed study measuring an outcome are not interchangeable forms of evidence. They can still illuminate the same research question.

## Workflow

### 1. State the question without preloading the answer

Prefer:

> What mechanisms concentrate decision power in this system, and with what consequences?

over:

> Why is this system obviously oppressive?

A question may originate from a strong prior belief. The method does not require pretending otherwise; it requires making the belief revisable.

### 2. Gather contrasting accounts

Seek contrast across **roles, incentives, experience, methods, and assumptions**, not merely political labels.

Useful contrasts may include:

- critic ↔ advocate;
- designer ↔ affected user;
- owner ↔ worker;
- institutional account ↔ participant account;
- stated goal ↔ observed result;
- historical expectation ↔ later outcome;
- one discipline's model ↔ another discipline's model.

### 3. Extract claims and assumptions

For each source, ask:

- What factual claims are being made?
- What causal mechanism is proposed?
- What values are assumed?
- What does the source treat as outside the system boundary?
- What would have to be true for this account to hold?
- What evidence would change the account?

### 4. Map convergence and divergence

Do not reduce the comparison to “side A / side B.”

Record:

- shared descriptive claims;
- conflicting factual claims;
- competing causal explanations;
- different definitions;
- value disagreements;
- different affected populations;
- different time horizons;
- unresolved uncertainty.

### 5. Route claims to stronger evidence

Contrast is a discovery tool, not the final arbiter.

Move factual claims toward the strongest relevant evidence and native methods:

- primary records where available;
- peer-reviewed research where appropriate;
- reliable datasets;
- reproducible technical evidence;
- legal texts and case records for legal claims;
- historical scholarship for historical claims;
- affected-party testimony where lived consequences are the object of inquiry.

### 6. Build the best-current model

The model should preserve:

- what appears well supported;
- what remains disputed;
- what is uncertain;
- which findings depend on values rather than facts;
- what evidence could falsify or materially revise the model.

### 7. Act without freezing the model

Research often informs design, policy, implementation, simulation, or further inquiry before certainty is possible.

Record review conditions so later evidence can reopen the conclusion.

## “Unintentional critique”

Sometimes an advocate of a system describes mechanisms that critics also identify as harmful.

That is analytically useful, but the method should resist turning it into a gotcha category. The stronger move is:

1. quote or summarize the advocate accurately;
2. identify the mechanism they are describing;
3. compare it with critics' and researchers' descriptions;
4. test the mechanism and consequences independently;
5. keep normative disagreement separate from descriptive convergence.

The interesting result may be:

> Both accounts describe concentrated control; they disagree about whether concentrated control is justified.

## Failure modes

Epistemic contrast fails when it becomes:

- **false balance:** treating weak and strong evidence as equivalent because viewpoints differ;
- **cherry-picked theater:** selecting unusually foolish advocates or critics to make one side look unserious;
- **role essentialism:** assuming owners, workers, critics, or experts all share one perspective;
- **perspective voting:** counting viewpoints instead of evaluating claims;
- **quote mining:** removing context to manufacture convergence or contradiction;
- **ideological laundering:** using “contrast” to avoid making evidence-weighted conclusions;
- **endless inquiry:** refusing to act because another perspective might exist.

## Relationship to deliberation

Epistemic contrast is usually upstream of [Deliberative Inquiry](deliberative-inquiry.md).

```text
contrast
  ↓
better shared map of the question
  ↓
deliberation
  ↓
judgment / action
```

A group cannot deliberate well if the available information already excludes relevant perspectives or disguises uncertainty.

## Working orientation

> **Contrast before convergence.**

The aim is not permanent disagreement. It is to make convergence, when it happens, more informative because relevant differences had a fair chance to expose what the current model was missing.
