# Pilot case: When is AI assistance genuinely free and accessible?

**Document role:** proposed case study / research design  
**Status:** not yet conducted; exploratory / author review pending

## Research question

Under what conditions can people *meaningfully benefit* from AI assistance without giving up disproportionate money, privacy, autonomy, time, expertise, or political influence?

"Free" can mean no price at the point of use, no licensing restriction, low technical overhead, no coerced personal-data exchange, ability to opt out, or freedom to modify and govern infrastructure. These properties must be distinguished, not bundled into one label.

## Cases to compare

1. A commercially operated hosted service with a no-cost access tier.
2. A publicly supported or nonprofit-operated hosted service.
3. A community-owned or cooperative shared service.
4. A locally operated open-weight model.

These are ideal types for comparison, not claims that any particular available product meets the description. Real systems may combine several arrangements.

## Questions by dimension

| Dimension | Concrete investigation |
| --- | --- |
| Access | Who can sign up, connect, afford necessary devices, and use supported languages and modalities? |
| Disability and usability | What accommodations exist, who tested them, and can people complete a task unassisted if desired? |
| Real benefit | Does the tool improve a participant-selected task or life opportunity, rather than merely generating outputs? |
| Agency | Can people refuse logging, select alternatives, export their work, leave, and meaningfully challenge decisions? |
| Governance | Who sets model objectives, use policies, safety boundaries, and access conditions? |
| Distribution | Who obtains the gains? Who supplies paid/unpaid labor? Who absorbs costs, including ecological burdens? |
| Comprehension | What level of explanation is necessary to give informed, usable choices without requiring technical mastery? |
| Repair | How are errors reported, addressed, compensated for, and used to change rules or deployments? |

## Research design

**Phase 1 — desk study:** compare publicly documented terms, pricing, availability, accessibility, governance, exit paths, environmental disclosures, and known limitations. Record missing information explicitly; do not equate silence with zero cost.

**Phase 2 — participatory protocol:** if consent, recruitment, safeguarding, and research permissions are established, invite diverse participants to define meaningful tasks and assess outcomes. No participant study is authorized merely by this document.

**Phase 3 — competing interpretations:** compare provider claims, accessibility expert analyses, worker and user accounts, institutional constraints, and independent evaluations. Use [Epistemic Contrast](../methods/epistemic-contrast.md). Findings may differ across people and settings.

**Phase 4 — bounded scenario modeling:** optionally model access and governance choices within a simulation. Treat the simulation as a test of assumptions, not predictive evidence or an ethics oracle.

## Counterexamples to seek

- A paid proprietary service that reliably improves substantive accessibility more than a nominally "free" alternative.
- A locally operated system whose privacy advantages coexist with severe hardware or support barriers.
- A public/community system that suffers from governance capture, underfunding, or weak reliability.
- A highly participatory process that is burdensome or merely symbolic.
- People who deliberately prefer convenience over a more autonomous configuration, and why.

## Non-goals

No unweighted universal "AI freedom score." No inference that technical openness is sufficient for human liberation. No claim that consent to a service proves legitimacy of upstream labor/resource impacts. No assumption that one study generalizes to all people or settings.
