# Research agenda — What If It All Works?

**Document role:** scoped inquiry plan; not a completed study  
**Status:** exploratory / AI-assisted / review pending  
**Method:** follow [Root Sequence Research Method](../method.md), particularly decompose → route to native fields → evidence → competing explanations → bounded test → revision.

## Primary question

Under what conditions do advances in knowledge, technology, and collective coordination become **meaningfully accessible, broadly shared, sustainable improvements in lives**, rather than simply achievements in technical performance or benefits for already advantaged groups?

## Secondary questions

1. Who has legitimate authority to specify "better," choose system boundaries, and declare success?
2. How can people benefit without mastering every underlying system, and without surrendering autonomy?
3. What can be measured and compared, and what remains omitted, contested, or irreducible to one metric?
4. Can societies make ethical progress without a universally settled notion of perfection?
5. How should care, ecological limits, future generations, and potentially nonhuman interests enter decisions?
6. What forms of institutional learning preserve both adaptability and enforceable commitments?

### Epistemic separation

**Conceptual/normative work** may clarify terms or defend obligations; it does not empirically establish that an institution works.  
**Empirical work** may document consequences and mechanisms; it does not by itself settle moral priorities.  
**Simulation** tests consequences of explicit assumptions; it does not prove the future.  
**First-person accounts** are vital evidence of experience, but no single account establishes every cause or distributional effect.

## Propositions to test, not adopt

- **H1 — Access gap.** Increasing the technical availability of a benefit does not necessarily proportionally increase meaningful access; conversion factors such as ability, knowledge, infrastructure, time, and governance may mediate the result.
- **H2 — Participation gap.** Consultation without decision rights, appeal, or recourse may leave affected people less influential than participation counts suggest.
- **H3 — Metric gap.** Aggregate welfare improvements can coexist with distributional harms that chosen measurements overlook.
- **H4 — Adaptation gap.** Institutions with better accessible feedback and correction mechanisms may respond more effectively to novel harms, but may also face instability or capture risks.
- **H5 — Anti-perfection risk.** Rejecting a final ideal may enable plural flourishing or may instead provide cover for avoidable harm, depending on accountability and substantive protections.

### What would weaken these claims?

Find cases where simply providing a benefit *does* reliably translate into meaningful access; where nonparticipatory expert governance clearly performs better under rights-respecting evaluation; where a limited set of metrics robustly captures the affected interests; where greater revisability undermines outcomes; or where consensus outperforms plural arrangements without coercion. Consider reverse causality, selection effects, institutional context, and feasibility constraints.

## An initial, bounded case

[Free and accessible AI?](case-study-ai-access.md) compares several ways people might receive AI assistance. It is a proposed study of access, dependency, governance, and material/economic burdens—not a recommendation that one hosting model is universally superior.

## Avoid misleading evaluation

Do not reduce the inquiry to a "perfection score," a universal moral ranking, or an unweighted six-item audit. Instead collect plural indicators, record tradeoffs and uncertainty, use disaggregated outcomes, and make participation/appeal procedures visible.

Candidate dimensions: technical performance, availability, affordability, accessibility, actual uptake, informed choice, control/exit, distribution of costs and labor, ecological impacts, challenge/repair mechanisms, and sustained usefulness. Selection of indicators is itself a governance decision.

## Critical counterquestions

- Who was missing from the definition of the problem and the design of the inquiry?
- Could the research unintentionally normalize scarcity, exploitation, or paternalism?
- Are costs really unavoidable, or only unavoidable under chosen rules?
- Is "complexity" being used to conceal power or avoid precise explanations?
- Do proposed safeguards obstruct urgent benefits, and how would that be detected?
- How could "everyone" be assessed without denying plural identities and interests?
- How can action proceed responsibly despite genuine disagreement and incomplete evidence?

## First practical outputs

1. Build a claim ledger: statement, type (empirical/normative/model), evidence, contradiction, uncertainty, responsible field.
2. Select one manageable institution or AI-access case; define the people and interests potentially affected, without assuming the boundary is complete.
3. Gather contrasting evidence from providers, workers, affected users, accessibility expertise, independent researchers, and regulators.
4. Compare at least two explanations and one realistic baseline; specify what would change the proposed model.
5. Publish a revision record separating findings from arguments, open questions, and decisions; do not silently promote an exploratory interpretation to canon.

## Potential project translations (not auto-assignments)

- **Liberated Intelligence:** agency, authority, privacy, and unequal access to powerful AI.
- **Being Human(e):** ordinary-life care, comprehension, dignity, support, and refusal.
- **Coherent World:** speculative stress tests of policies and infrastructures; remain within its own access rules.
- **Common Future Compact / policy work:** institutional proposals subject to cost, rights, and feasibility analysis.
- **Existential Euphoria:** the experience of finitude, contradictions, meaning, and openness; not a substitute for evidence.
