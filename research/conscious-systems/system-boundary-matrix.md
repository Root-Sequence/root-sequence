# CS-Q16 — Where Is the Conscious System?

**Document role:** Fourth bounded research pass<br>
**Status:** v0.1-dev; AI-assisted, author review pending<br>
**Evidence status:** Selected primary-study evidence and conceptual distinctions; not a systematic review or a demonstrated minimal substrate

## Provisional answer

For this human-focused research program, use interacting brain networks within a physiologically supported organism as the initial measurement frame. Do not interpret that practical choice as proof that the entire brain, entire body, or surrounding environment constitutes each experience. The minimum sufficient system remains unresolved.

The crucial distinction is between **causal dependence and constitution**. If changing something changes experience, it has a relevant causal role; that alone does not tell us whether it is part of the experience-producing mechanism, an input to it, its support system, or its means of reporting.

This qualifies our whole-system hypothesis: coupling is a reason to investigate a boundary, not evidence that everything coupled belongs to one experiencer. Conversely, a brain-centered measurement is not proof that bodily processes are dispensable.

## Four boundaries to record separately

| Boundary | Question | What must not be inferred automatically |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Enabling support | What maintains the conditions under which experience can occur? | Every necessary support process is a constituent of experience |
| Constitutive mechanism | Which interacting processes realize this experience during the target interval? | A correlated region, or one effective stimulation site, is the complete mechanism |
| Content influence | What determines or changes what is experienced? | Every source of information is inside the experiencer |
| Evidence and access | What permits action, report, memory, or an observer's measurement? | Loss of this channel establishes loss of experience |

These are explanatory roles, not necessarily separate anatomical compartments. A structure may perform several roles. Always specify the target: any experience, one particular content, bodily selfhood, access, or unity of subject.

## Candidate spatial boundaries

| Candidate | Why investigate it? | What would strengthen the boundary claim? | Main unresolved alternative |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Local recurrent circuit | Could realize a particular perceptual content | Content-specific causal effects with upstream input, arousal, and downstream report effects separated | The circuit changes content in a larger conscious system but is not independently conscious |
| Distributed cortical network | Could coordinate contents and flexible access | Manipulations of cross-region interaction predict experiential changes beyond local activity or task demand | The measured network mainly enables memory, decision, and report |
| Thalamocortical and arousal-related systems | Could determine whether content-supporting dynamics remain possible | Reversible interventions change state alongside network interactions | A control node regulates a mechanism located elsewhere; arousal is not a complete experience measure |
| Brain-body system | Bodily regulation and signals may contribute to affect, selfhood, or broader experience | Selective bodily manipulations distinguish informational, metabolic, and constitutive roles | The body supports or supplies input to neural mechanisms rather than extending their constitutive boundary |
| Organism-environment loop | Ongoing action and sensory feedback may be essential for particular experiences | Live coupling produces effects not explained by matched sensory input, action demands, or expectation | The environment changes the experience's causes and contents without becoming part of its subject |

These are candidate models, not increasingly conscious levels. Larger is not automatically better; local and distributed accounts can also describe different aspects of the same episode.

## What selected studies establish

### A local intervention can alter content without locating the whole subject

Parvizi and colleagues electrically stimulated face-selective fusiform regions in one implanted patient. Stimulation distorted the appearance of faces, with sham and nearby-site controls.[^parvizi] This supplies causal evidence for a contribution to face perception. It does not show that the stimulated region alone experiences faces: the rest of the functioning brain and body remained present, and stimulation can affect connected networks.

### A small control site can influence a distributed state

Redinbaugh and colleagues recorded thalamic and cortical activity across wakefulness, sleep, and anesthesia in macaques. Central lateral thalamic stimulation restored arousal and wake-like cortical dynamics under selected stimulation conditions.[^redinbaugh] This supports a causal regulatory role within a network, not a claim that consciousness resides in the thalamus alone. Behavioral and neural evidence in nonhuman animals remains indirect evidence of subjective experience.

### Bodily signals matter, but their role needs qualification

Park and colleagues found that pre-stimulus neural responses locked to heartbeats predicted detection of faint visual targets.[^park] The relevant association was with the brain's response to bodily signals, not simply heart rate. This motivates investigating interoceptive contributions; it does not establish that the heart constitutes visual experience or that heartbeat processing is necessary for all consciousness. The study is observational, not a bodily-removal or replacement experiment.

### Cutting a connection does not provide a simple count of subjects

Pinto and colleagues studied two callosotomy patients and reported impaired comparison across visual fields alongside response abilities less strictly divided by hand or verbal output than a simple textbook account predicts.[^pinto] Much of the follow-up testing involved one patient. Their interpretation favors conscious unity, but the behavioral result does not itself count subjects. Residual routes, compensation, and response strategies must be considered. Neither anatomical separation nor coordinated responding alone establishes whether there is one experiential field or two.

## Boundary tests: useful, but not decisive by themselves

1. **Disruption:** If removing a contribution abolishes a report, first distinguish content loss, arousal loss, motor impairment, and memory failure. Disruption identifies dependence under tested conditions, not automatically constitution.
2. **Replacement or rescue:** If a function can be restored by a different implementation, that weakens the necessity of the original material implementation under those conditions. The replacement may become a new constituent; rescue does not prove that the replaced role was merely external support.
3. **Disconnection:** Compare within-part and across-part information use, with independent input and response channels. A communication bottleneck is not yet a boundary between subjects.
4. **Live coupling versus replay:** Match sensory histories while changing whether feedback responds to the participant. Differences can identify a role for closed-loop interaction; unmatched expectation, agency, attention, or motor signals remain alternatives to environmental constitution.

These are analytic designs for reviewing existing evidence and developing predictions, not instructions for self-experimentation. Clinical stimulation, anesthesia, lesions, and animal interventions require qualified teams and appropriate ethical oversight.

## Time and history are boundary choices too

A brief perceptual episode, an arousal state, and the developmental history that made a brain possible are different targets. Historical necessity does not by itself put the whole past inside the present mechanism. Equally, a snapshot may omit ongoing feedback needed to explain the episode.

For every proposed boundary, record: target phenomenon; interval; included components; assumed external support and inputs; measured interactions; intervention; predicted experiential change; report and memory effects; competing explanation. State what evidence would force expansion or contraction of the boundary.

## Correction to the previous proposed experiment

The transition matrix suggested that local recurrence followed by a valid surprise report could challenge GNW if broad availability was absent. This needs a temporal qualification: the probe could itself cause retrieval and global availability, or reconstruct an experience judgment. A later report cannot establish that broad availability was absent at the original interval, nor that the reported content was experienced before the probe.

The comparison therefore requires time-resolved pre-probe measurements and explicit models of retrieval and reconstruction. Even then, the inference remains theory-dependent. No-report designs reduce one confound while making the evidence for experience less direct.

## Decisions and next deliverable

- Retain a brain-centered, physiologically embedded measurement frame for the initial human perceptual comparison; do not claim a proven brain-only sufficient substrate.
- Separate mechanisms for particular contents, overall conscious capacity, and unity of subject. One boundary need not answer all three.
- Treat body and environment as candidate contributors whose roles require evidence, not automatic inclusions or exclusions.
- Keep extrapolation to other animals or artificial systems explicit and conditional.
- Next: **CS-Q26–28, an inference-audit protocol** for the proposed perceptual comparison. Specify every observation, its rival explanation, the temporal assumptions, and the result that would change confidence before collecting data or declaring a theory favored.
