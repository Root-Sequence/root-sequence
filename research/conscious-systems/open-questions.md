# Conscious Systems — Open Questions

**Document role:** Living question and decision ledger<br>
**Status:** v0.1-dev; AI-assisted research scaffold, author review pending<br>
**Evidence status:** Questions and provisional positions; not findings

This ledger turns the broad question *“What can we change about a mind while preserving consciousness?”* into smaller questions that can be defined, compared, and tested. A question belongs here when answering it would change the research program, an experiment, or an ethical judgment.

## Current working positions

These positions record the inquiry's present direction. They remain revisable.

1. **Physical dependence:** Human consciousness depends on an organized physical system. This is a dependence claim, not yet a complete explanation or an identity claim.
2. **Coupled system:** Chemistry, electrical signaling, metabolism, anatomy, bodily regulation, environment, feedback, development, and history constrain one another. The necessary system boundary remains open.
3. **Separate variables:** A physical event, subjective experience, experience content, responsiveness, memory of occurrence, memory of content, implicit traces, and later report are different variables.
4. **Plural subconscious:** “Subconscious” is an umbrella for several processes and access relations, not evidence of one hidden agent or location.
5. **Theory comparison:** Candidate theories should make risky, divergent predictions. Agreement with one measurement does not establish necessity or sufficiency.
6. **Ethical separation:** Consciousness, moral consideration, personhood, capability, and legitimate authority require separate judgments.
7. **Death/consciousness separation:** Life, death, current experience, capacity for experience, responsiveness, and recoverability are different variables. Neither absent experience nor absent report is by itself death.

Positions 2–4 and 7 originate in the initiating discussion and follow-up author clarifications. Their formulation here is AI-assisted and awaiting review.

## A. What phenomenon are we trying to explain?

| ID | Question | Why it matters | First route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CS-Q01 | What is the smallest definition of subjective experience that does not depend on report, intelligence, selfhood, or memory? | Every experiment otherwise changes its target midstream. | Compare phenomenal, access, state, and self-consciousness definitions; write inclusion and exclusion cases. |
| CS-Q02 | Is consciousness one property or a family of partly separable dimensions? | Wakefulness, content, connectedness, selfhood, affect, access, and metacognition can vary separately. | Build a multidimensional state description instead of one conscious/unconscious label. |
| CS-Q03 | Are experience and conscious access the same event? | Some theories explain global availability while others target phenomenality itself. | Find cases where experience is claimed without broad access, and specify what would count as evidence. |
| CS-Q04 | Does every experience have content, or can there be contentless awareness? | A positive answer changes what neural comparisons and reports must detect. | Review sleep, meditation, seizure, anesthesia, and minimal-phenomenology work with strict report limits. |
| CS-Q05 | Is affect or valence necessary for experience, or one possible dimension of it? | This directly affects theories of animal and artificial welfare. | Compare sensory content, pain, affective blunting, and alleged neutral awareness. |

## B. What changes processing into experience?

| ID | Question | Why it matters | First route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CS-Q06 | What distinguishes a nonconscious representation from an experienced one? | This is the central transition question. | Compare recurrence, global broadcast, higher-order representation, causal integration, and predictive-model accounts. |
| CS-Q07 | Is the transition graded, abrupt, or scale-dependent? | Threshold and graded theories predict different trial-level dynamics. | Use near-threshold stimuli and model individual trials rather than only averages. |
| CS-Q08 | Does conscious processing add a causal capacity that matched nonconscious processing lacks? | It tests whether consciousness changes what the system can do. | Hold task performance or stimulus information constant while testing flexible transfer, planning, or cross-system use. |
| CS-Q09 | Can the same content move between nonconscious, preconscious, and conscious states without changing its representational format? | This distinguishes changes in strength or access from changes in code. | Track content-specific neural representations through masking, attention, and report manipulations. |
| CS-Q10 | Which processes grouped as “subconscious” are constitutive of consciousness, and which merely influence it? | Causes, background conditions, contents, and consequences should not be conflated. | Test each proposed process against the six meanings listed in the opening paper. |

## C. Time, memory, and self

| ID | Question | Why it matters | First route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CS-Q11 | What is the shortest possible experience? | Consciousness may require temporal integration rather than an instantaneous state. | Compare temporal-threshold, masking, and interruption experiments across modalities. |
| CS-Q12 | What produces apparent continuity despite changing contents and interrupted awareness? | Continuity may be constructed through memory, prediction, and bodily stability. | Compare sleep, anesthesia, seizures, amnesia, and perceptual discontinuities. |
| CS-Q13 | Can an experience occur without forming any later retrievable memory? | If yes, retrospective report systematically undercounts experience. | Combine immediate probes, delayed probes, physiology, and memory disruption while avoiding report-only inference. |
| CS-Q14 | What is preserved when someone remembers only that an experience occurred? | Occurrence memory and content memory may depend on different traces. | Design reports that separately test confidence in occurrence, content, source, and reconstruction. |
| CS-Q15 | Is a self-model required for any experience, or only for experience represented as happening to “me”? | Bare phenomenality and self-consciousness may have different conditions. | Compare depersonalization, body-ownership illusions, meditation, infancy, and nonhuman evidence. |

## D. Where is the relevant system boundary?

| ID | Question | Why it matters | First route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CS-Q16 | Which spatial scale is necessary: local circuit, distributed brain network, whole brain, body, or organism-environment loop? | The answer determines what must be preserved across injury, replacement, or artificial implementation. | Test nested interventions and record which dimensions of experience change at each scale. |
| CS-Q17 | Are metabolism and homeostasis merely support conditions, or part of the mechanism of experience? | Biological and artificial systems may differ most sharply here. | Compare state changes caused by metabolic, neuromodulatory, and informational interventions with similar behavioral effects. |
| CS-Q18 | How much does interoception contribute to basic experience, affect, selfhood, and continuity? | Bodily signaling may shape some dimensions without being necessary for all content. | Manipulate or compare access to internal bodily signals while separating sensory content from self-related experience. |
| CS-Q19 | Does development and learned history partly constitute a conscious system, or merely configure its present state? | A present-state copy may not reproduce a historically formed process. | Compare developmental compensation, sudden lesions, gradual substitution, and trained artificial systems. |
| CS-Q20 | Can part of a system be conscious independently of the whole, producing multiple overlapping or competing fields? | This bears on split brains, local experience, dissociation, and distributed systems. | Define predictions for integration, cross-access, conflict, and independent report channels. |

## E. Which properties generalize across implementations?

| ID | Question | Why it matters | First route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CS-Q21 | Which candidate properties recur across mammals, birds, cephalopods, insects, and other nervous systems? | Convergence across different biology can separate broad organization from species-specific anatomy. | Use theory-specific predictions and matched behavioral alternatives rather than a single intelligence scale. |
| CS-Q22 | What biological property would a nonbiological conscious system specifically need to reproduce? | “Biology matters” needs a mechanism, while “substrate independent” needs an equivalence criterion. | List candidate roles for chemistry, cellular dynamics, metabolism, plasticity, and embodiment; design replacement arguments for each. |
| CS-Q23 | What would make two systems causally equivalent enough for a consciousness inference? | Matching behavior or software description may leave internal causal organization different. | Compare intervention responses, counterfactual structure, temporal dynamics, learning history, and embodiment. |
| CS-Q24 | Could an artificial system have experience without human-like language, self-report, or agency? | Language-centered tests risk both false positives and false negatives. | Derive nonlinguistic predictions from each theory before examining any system. |
| CS-Q25 | Could a collective or environmentally distributed system support one field of experience? | Coordination, memory, and intelligence across parts do not automatically imply one subject. | Specify required causal integration, temporal unity, boundaries, and interventions that would distinguish one subject from coordinated many. |

## F. Evidence, experiments, and ethics

| ID | Question | Why it matters | First route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CS-Q26 | How can a study distinguish consciousness from attention, decision, action, memory, and report? | These processes often occur together and contaminate proposed markers. | Combine report, no-report, perturbation, and delayed-memory conditions in one design. |
| CS-Q27 | What evidence can establish necessity, sufficiency, or only correlation? | A reliable marker may still be downstream from consciousness. | Require causal intervention, converging interventions, and explicit rival explanations. |
| CS-Q28 | What observation would count against each theory? | A theory that accommodates every outcome cannot narrow the possibility space. | Preregister theory-specific failure conditions with proponents and neutral investigators. |
| CS-Q29 | How should uncertainty about consciousness affect treatment of animals, patients, organoids, and artificial systems? | Waiting for certainty may permit harm; indiscriminate attribution also has costs. | Develop graded evidence and welfare precautions without converting them into claims of personhood or authority. |
| CS-Q30 | Which dimensions of possible experience create welfare risk? | Capacity for suffering may depend on valence, persistence, memory, helplessness, and self-modeling rather than consciousness alone. | Track welfare-relevant capacities separately and examine whether architectures can avoid or limit them. |

## G. What can dying and death reveal?

Death is not defined here as the opposite of consciousness. Organisms can be alive without current experience or, perhaps, without ever having supported experience; loss of responsiveness can be reversible; and biological processes end at different rates across cells, organs, and the organism. The scientifically useful target is therefore the **process by which a once-conscious system loses, retains, or regains particular capacities**, not “death” as one instantaneous switch.

| ID | Question | Why it matters | First route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CS-Q31 | How should organismal life and death be separated from current experience, capacity for experience, responsiveness, and recoverability? | Treating these as one binary state confuses sleep, anesthesia, coma, cardiac arrest, brain death, and cellular survival. | Build a state matrix using independent biological, behavioral, experiential, and reversibility dimensions. |
| CS-Q32 | What distinguishes reversible loss of consciousness from permanent loss of the capacity for consciousness? | The contrast may identify enabling conditions more clearly than ordinary conscious/unconscious comparisons. | Compare anesthesia, sleep, syncope, coma, cardiac arrest with resuscitation, and death by neurologic criteria while controlling for drugs and injury. |
| CS-Q33 | In what order do metabolism, circulation, brainstem regulation, large-scale coordination, content-specific processing, access, and report fail during dying? | The order of breakdown could discriminate causal layers from downstream markers. | Align physiological and neural time series around circulatory arrest, hypoxia, withdrawal of support, and successful resuscitation; avoid assuming every terminal signal is experiential. |
| CS-Q34 | Can transient organized brain activity during cardiac arrest or terminal hypoxia support experience, or is it disinhibited, pathological, or fragmented activity? | Gamma power or connectivity can resemble proposed correlates of consciousness without demonstrating experience. | Compare terminal dynamics with verified conscious, anesthetized, seizure, hypoxic, and recovery states; require converging evidence and theory-specific predictions. |
| CS-Q35 | What does preserved or restored cellular, synaptic, metabolic, or circulatory activity after organismal death reveal about the minimum organization required for consciousness? | Surviving parts show that cellular activity, whole-organ coordination, and consciousness must not be treated as equivalent. | Compare local recovery with global electrocorticographic organization, perturbational responses, integration, and the possibility of coordinated brain-body regulation. |
| CS-Q36 | Which features of dying generalize across humans, other conscious animals, organisms of uncertain consciousness, and living systems that may never be conscious? | Death is common to life, whereas consciousness may not be; many death mechanisms may reveal self-maintenance rather than experience. | Separate conserved cellular and organismal failure mechanisms from evidence specifically linked to conscious capacity across taxa. |

### Initial evidence boundaries

- The 2023 United States consensus guideline defines brain death/death by neurologic criteria as permanent loss of function of the brain as a whole, including the brainstem, resulting in coma, brainstem areflexia, and apnea under adequate testing conditions.[^brain-death] It is not merely unresponsiveness or an ordinary unconscious state.
- BrainEx experiments restored microcirculation and several molecular, cellular, metabolic, and spontaneous synaptic functions in isolated pig brains four hours post-mortem without global electrocorticographic activity.[^brainex] This separates cellular viability and local activity from demonstrated whole-brain organization or consciousness.
- A retrospective study of four comatose dying patients found surges of gamma power and connectivity after withdrawal of ventilatory support in two patients.[^xu] The sample was small, both responders had seizure histories, no reports were possible, and a consciousness-associated signal is not evidence that experience occurred.
- AWARE-II studied in-hospital cardiac arrest during resuscitation. Of 567 arrests, 53 people survived, 28 completed interviews, and 11 reported memories or perceptions the authors classified as suggestive of consciousness; EEG patterns interpreted as compatible with consciousness also appeared during some prolonged resuscitations.[^aware] These are observations during potentially reversible arrest and recovery among survivors, not reports from irreversible death.

2. **CS-Q06:** map proposed transitions from processing to experience.
