# Coherence Model

**Document role:** Developing systems model for examining alignment, mismatch, feedback, and revisability over time  
**Status:** AI-assisted revision for author review, 2026-09-24  
**Scope:** Cross-domain analytical model; not a universal score, scientific law, or automatic moral judgment  
**Related concept:** [Dynamic Coherence and Adaptive Continuity](../../concepts/dynamic-coherence.md)

Coherence is provisionally understood here as **the quality of fit among a system's structure, assumptions, operating conditions, feedback, purposes, relationships, and consequences within a stated boundary and timescale**.

The earlier model emphasized whether assumptions matched reality. That remains important, but it is too static by itself for systems that learn, adapt, reorganize, externalize costs, or change the conditions in which they operate.

---

## Core terms

### Assumption

A condition the system behaves as though it were true.

An assumption can be explicit, implicit, inherited, encoded in infrastructure, or visible only when it fails.

### Environment

The conditions outside the chosen system boundary that influence the system or are influenced by it.

The boundary is analytical, not proof that externalized effects are irrelevant.

### Alignment

A context-specific fit among relevant assumptions, structures, signals, actions, conditions, and consequences.

Alignment in one dimension does not establish overall coherence.

### Mismatch

A consequential divergence between what the system assumes, senses, optimizes, promises, or requires and the conditions or effects that actually obtain.

### Feedback

Information or consequences that can change later behavior.

Feedback can be delayed, distorted, ignored, suppressed, expensive to provide, or available only to actors without power to act on it.

### Revisability

The practical capacity to detect consequential mismatch and change assumptions, rules, structure, behavior, or goals when warranted.

Revisability includes the ability to challenge the model used to define the mismatch.

### Dynamic coherence

A working term for coherence assessed across change and time rather than at one snapshot.

It asks whether a system can remain responsive to conditions and consequences while preserving important capabilities and avoiding local success that destroys wider or longer-term viability.

---

## Static fit and dynamic coherence

A snapshot can be coherent in a limited sense:

    assumption matches condition
    action achieves intended local result

But a system can still fail dynamically if:

- the condition is changing faster than the system can update;
- feedback arrives only after irreversible damage;
- success creates a dependency that later blocks adaptation;
- a local solution shifts costs outside the chosen boundary;
- the system cannot acknowledge or repair error;
- alternatives disappear as the successful path becomes locked in;
- power prevents affected people from challenging the operating model.

Dynamic coherence therefore adds temporal questions:

> What happens when conditions change?

> What happens when the system is wrong?

> Who notices?

> Who can respond?

> Which capabilities and relationships survive the correction?

> What future possibilities does the current solution open or close?

---

## Coherence is not maximum order

Order, consistency, stability, and optimization can support coherence, but none is equivalent to it.

A system may be extremely orderly because:

- dissent is suppressed;
- alternatives are prohibited;
- feedback is filtered;
- exit is impossible;
- one metric dominates;
- uncertainty is hidden;
- affected people are excluded from the boundary.

Likewise, a flexible system can be incoherent if it cannot maintain commitments, shared protocols, memory, safety constraints, or reliable function.

The question is not:

> stability or change?

It is:

> **What should remain stable, what must remain changeable, under whose authority, and how can the system recognize when the balance no longer fits its conditions?**

---

## Multi-scale coherence

The same process can look different at different scales.

A change may be:

- effective for one component;
- costly for another;
- stable for an organization;
- fragile for the infrastructure supporting it;
- beneficial immediately;
- damaging after repeated use;
- locally adaptive;
- systemically destructive.

Every strong coherence claim should therefore name:

- **boundary:** what is inside the assessment;
- **scale:** component, relationship, organization, network, ecosystem, society, other;
- **timescale:** immediate, short, medium, long, intergenerational, other;
- **purpose or need:** what the process is supposed to accomplish;
- **affected parties:** including people outside the formal system;
- **externalities:** burdens or benefits moved elsewhere;
- **uncertainty:** what is not known.

“Coherent” without those qualifiers risks becoming a mood rather than an analysis.

---

## Coherence and adaptation

Adaptation can increase coherence when it reduces a real mismatch.

It can also reduce coherence when it:

- optimizes around a bad proxy;
- learns from distorted feedback;
- preserves the system by shifting costs;
- hardens an emergency response after conditions change;
- removes alternatives;
- increases dependence on one actor;
- hides the evidence that correction is needed.

See the [Adaptation model](../adaptation/model.md) for a more explicit analysis of state change, transition dynamics, path dependence, and possibility-space effects.

---

## Coherence and agency

A system can achieve its target while reducing meaningful agency.

Examples of mechanisms include:

- making refusal impractical;
- eliminating interoperable alternatives;
- concentrating decision rights;
- making consequences illegible;
- increasing switching costs;
- requiring affected people to absorb adaptation costs;
- restricting appeal or correction.

Agency is not the only value and cannot always be maximized for every actor simultaneously. Constraints can protect other people's safety or agency.

The analytical requirement is to record the tradeoff rather than calling target success sufficient evidence of coherence.

The cross-project [Dynamic Coherence and Adaptive Continuity](../../concepts/dynamic-coherence.md) framework uses **agency envelope** as provisional language for effective reachable possibilities. That term remains to be operationalized.

---

## Coherence and power

Mismatch does not automatically generate correction.

Ask:

- Who can define the problem?
- Who chooses the metric?
- Who receives feedback?
- Who can dismiss it?
- Who controls resources needed for change?
- Who can veto alternatives?
- Who bears the costs while revision is delayed?
- Who benefits from treating the present arrangement as stable, natural, or inevitable?

A system that can force its environment to absorb every mismatch may look internally stable while becoming increasingly incoherent at a wider boundary.

---

## Coherence and false coherence

The [Coherent Systems living paper](../../research/papers/coherent-systems/README.md) develops **false coherence** for cases where apparent effectiveness depends on excluded people, power, consequences, or assumptions.

This model should remain compatible with that work without pre-empting the paper's evidence standards.

A useful warning sign is:

> The system “works” only because the assessment excludes whoever or whatever absorbs the failure.

---

## Properties

Coherence may be:

- explicit or implicit;
- local or systemic;
- temporary or persistent;
- visible or hidden;
- relational rather than internal;
- stable or drifting;
- improving in one dimension while deteriorating in another;
- contested among affected parties;
- difficult to summarize without losing consequential disagreement.

Do not assume coherence is binary or reducible to one scalar.

---

## Guiding questions

### Conditions and assumptions

- What does the system assume?
- Which assumptions are explicit, inherited, or embedded in infrastructure?
- What current conditions support or contradict them?

### Feedback and learning

- Which consequences become visible?
- Which remain hidden or delayed?
- Who receives feedback?
- Who has authority and resources to respond?
- What happens after the system discovers it was wrong?

### Time and history

- Which earlier states constrain current possibilities?
- What temporary workaround became permanent?
- What dependencies or lock-in did earlier success create?
- Which response was adaptive under a previous condition but costly now?

### Agency and power

- Who can participate, refuse, leave, appeal, repair, or modify?
- Which possibilities are technically available but practically inaccessible?
- Whose agency is widened or narrowed by the change?
- Who controls the conditions of future adaptation?

### Scale and externalities

- At what scale does the system appear coherent?
- What happens when the boundary expands?
- Who or what absorbs costs outside the local model?
- Does one system's resilience depend on another system's fragility?

### Revisability

- Can the system change without losing essential capabilities?
- Can a decision be reversed?
- Can the operating model itself be challenged?
- What would count as evidence that the present arrangement should be retired?

## Principle

> **Coherence is not the absence of change. For adaptive systems, a stronger test is whether consequential mismatch can remain visible and revisable across time, scale, power, and changing conditions without preserving local order by destroying the wider capacity to respond.**

This principle is a working analytical direction, not a demonstrated universal law.
